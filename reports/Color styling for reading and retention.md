# Sparse, meaningful styling beats colorful layers

**The evidence supports a narrow version of your tool and does not support the broad one.** What survives scrutiny: a small number of accurately chosen emphasis marks on content that matters improves memory for the marked material (instructor-provided highlighting, about 0.44 effect size, abstract-level) ([Ponce et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09654-1)), signaling meta-analyses agree on small-to-moderate gains (g+ 0.53 retention, 0.33 transfer) ([Schneider et al. 2018](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X17300581)), and for a grapheme-color synesthete the one firm design rule is to personalize colors, because mappings are stable within a person and idiosyncratic across people ([Carmichael et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5047354)). What does not survive: automatic multi-category coloring of prose by grammar or content type has no controlled support for typical adult English readers, and the one place your tool is most distinctive, an AI choosing what to emphasize, has never been tested. For you specifically, there is no study of coloured-text tools for synesthetes, no study of how externally rendered colors interact with a person's own letter colors during real reading, and no dose-response data on how many cues or how much styling is optimal. The practical consequence is that the design should be built around sparsity, accuracy of selection, a handful of colors each paired with a non-color cue, strict contrast checks, a frozen personal mapping, and built-in instrumentation, so that you can run the experiments that the literature has not. The notes are a rapid web pass, not a systematic review; many figures are abstract-level or secondary and are flagged where used.

## The evidence ladder: what is strong, moderate, weak, and absent

The table separates what a design decision can lean on from what it cannot. "Abstract-level" and "secondary" flags are carried over from the notes.

Two cautions apply to every row above. First, most positive results are for memory of the marked material under a test aimed at that material, not for overall comprehension; Dunlosky rates highlighting's generalizability to criterion tasks as "Negative" ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)). Second, the signaling and color-coding positives come from instructional multimedia, so they apply to your tool by analogy.

## Your synesthesia: personalize the palette, expect no memory dividend

For a grapheme-color synesthete the research gives one actionable certainty and several things to avoid assuming. The certainty is that your letter colors are stable (consistent matches were reported even with test-retest intervals approaching 8 years, though the notes could not confirm the authors of that paper) ([PMC3648671](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3648671/figure/F1)) and that other people's mappings will differ from yours, with only weak shared tendencies such as A often red or initial-letter effects (b to blue, y to yellow) that also appear when non-synesthetes are forced to choose ([MIT Open Encyclopedia, secondary](https://oecs.knowledgefutures.org/pub/rma3jjdk); [Simner et al. 2005 via lecture slides, secondary](https://www.inf.ed.ac.uk/teaching/courses/cnl/CNL_08_9_synaesthesia.pdf)). Any default palette will therefore clash with your internal colors on most letters.

What a clash costs is partly known. In naming tasks, incongruent printed color slows responses automatically; congruent primes gave a 79 ms benefit over incongruent in 19 synesthetes, with no effect in 19 controls ([van Leeuwen et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2919410)). Whether that interference touches fluent reading, comprehension or memory is unstudied, and the notes flag that Stroop-like interference does not by itself show the colour is perceptual, since it can arise at semantic or decisional levels ([Hubbard and Ramachandran review via search summary, secondary](https://www.sfu.ca/~kathleea/docs/2005%20-%20Hubbard%20&%20Rama%20-%20Neurocog%20Mechs%20of%20Syn%20copy.pdf)). The notes also report a mixed picture on whether internal colors help perception: some benefit for texture segregation and crowding in six synesthetes ([Hubbard et al. 2005](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15797557/)) but no difference in a visual-search study ([City repository, abstract-level](https://city-test.eprints-hosting.org/id/eprint/14911/1/Synaesthetic%20colours%20do%20not%20camouflage%20form%20in%20visual%20search..pdf)). A worry specific to your tool, and an inference rather than a finding, is that word-level semantic colors (for example, "this is a claim") sit on top of letter-level photisms, so your own letter colors and the tool's meaning colors compete for the same visual channel. That competition has never been measured.

Do not count on a memory dividend from rendering your own colors. Synesthetes' memory advantage is real but modest and general: it showed up for words, non-words, scenes and fractals, largest for achromatic fractals, so it is not tied to synaesthetically colored material ([Ward et al. 2013](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00762/full)); several follow-up effects are borderline (p about .05) and only the pure grapheme-color group showed a persistent advantage at a year-plus retest ([Lunke and Meier 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7044321/)). Displaying your own colors is, by analogy to the priming result, at worst neutral for color-dimension tasks, and no reading-speed benefit is documented. Training non-synesthetes with colored letters produces Stroop-like effects, fading phenomenology and unverified reading or memory benefits ([Bor et al. 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4235407/), [Schwartzman et al. 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6834010)), which matters here only as a warning: a tool that systematically colors text on screen is also a training regimen for whatever mapping it uses. Do not let the tool teach you a second, competing letter or word mapping by accident.

## Sparse, accurate emphasis works; dense or wrong emphasis erodes itself

The highlighting literature explains most of what should govern an auto-styling tool. Dunlosky's low rating reflects how students use highlighting (too much, unselective), and the mechanism that does work is the isolation (von Restorff) effect: marked text pops out, and over-marking destroys the distinctiveness ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)). The sparse protocols that helped were concrete: **one underlined sentence per paragraph** (Rickards and August 1975; L. L. Johnson 1988) and **five critical statements per article** marked by the experimenter (Cashen and Leicht 1970), the latter with no cost to adjacent sentences, all as reviewed by Dunlosky. Because selection quality is the lever the tool controls, this is the best argument for it: tool-chosen emphasis could capture the instructor-provided benefit (about 0.44 on memory and comprehension, versus 0.36 and a non-significant 0.20 for learner-generated, abstract-level) ([Ponce et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09654-1)) if its selections resemble an expert's.

The failure mode is equally documented. Pre-existing inappropriate highlighting, that is, marks on the wrong passages, lowered comprehension and metacomprehension (abstract-level) ([Gier et al. 2011](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/the-effects-of-pre-existing-inappropriate-highlighting-on-reading-comprehension/811806564355670018-25405), [Gier et al. 2010](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/02702710903397082)). So a wrong AI emphasis is not neutral; the evidence says it can hurt. Other caveats: marked material gains while unmarked material shows a small cost in one study (Fowler and Barker) and none in others, and Peterson (1992) found worse inference performance for underliners at one week and two months, flagged by Dunlosky as needing replication ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)). Digital screens may blunt the benefit (single abstract-level study, [Ben-Yehudah and Eshet-Alkalai 2018](https://cris.openu.ac.il/en/publications/the-contribution-of-text-highlighting-to-comprehension-a-comparis/)), and screens already carry a small comprehension cost for expository text under time pressure (54-study meta-analysis; effect size not captured) ([Delgado et al. 2018](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/dont-throw-away-your-printed-books-a-meta-analysis-on-the-effects/)). The notes also record that generation helps memory in general (d about 0.40) ([Bertsch et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/)) and that students skeptical of highlighting benefited more than believers in one abstract-level study ([Yue et al. 2015](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/highlighting-and-its-relation-to-distributed-study-and-students-metacognitive-beliefs/814713310267047937-10463)); an "AI suggests, you accept or edit" flow might preserve the generation benefit, but that is untested.

Where the evidence ends is the dose. No study manipulates the percentage of text emphasized and measures comprehension; "emphasis density has an optimum" is labelled untested in the notes. The only density hints are indirect: start-bolding about half of every word was rated less readable than plain text (7.48 plain vs 6.76 start-bolded vs 5.43 middle-bolded) with no performance gain ([Parker et al. 2026 via ScienceBlog write-up](https://scienceblog.com/s-bionic-reading-bolds-the-first-half-of-each-word-on-the-promise-of-more-efficient-reading-and-a-registered-eye-tracking-study-of-90-skilled-adult-readers-found-the-bolding-moved-their-first/); paper page blocked, existence confirmed at [Reading CentAUR](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/130289/)), and bolding only content words gave about +3 points keyword memory but worse factual-question performance in a Khmer preprint ([Buoy et al. 2026, not peer reviewed](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00688v1)). Finally, stylistic emphasis is not a substitute for retrieval practice or spacing, which Dunlosky rates high ([Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)).

## Layer by layer: only meaning and linkage have a defensible rationale

**Document structure (headings, bullets, bold key terms).** Supported as scanning aids, not as comprehension or retention aids. NN/g's eye tracking (232 users) describes F-shaped scanning but not thoroughness, and its "124%" usability gain for scannable, concise, objective text is a 1997 single-site result that confounds headings, bullets, highlighted keywords and half the word count ([NN/g F-pattern](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/), [Morkes and Nielsen](https://www.nngroup.com/alertbox/9710a.html)). Hue is a preattentive feature, found in near-constant time regardless of distractor count, while conjunctions of cues are not ([Healey and Enns 2012](https://visionlab-psych.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2015/06/140_HealeyEnns_IEEE2011.pdf)); that supports one unique cue per target class for finding things quickly, but skimming benefit is not retention benefit.

**Content types (code, definitions, quotes, numbers, examples).** Two separate bodies of evidence. Syntax highlighting in code is mixed: null in the largest experiment (390 novices) ([Hannebauer et al. 2018](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/ese/HannebauerHG18.html)), non-significant in a student eye-tracking study ([Beelders and du Plessis 2015](https://bop.unibe.ch/JEMR/article/view/2429)), and faster task completion in one 10-person study whose benefit shrank with experience ([Sarkar 2015](https://advait.org/files/sarkar_2015_syntax_colouring.pdf)); nothing tests transfer to prose. Color that maps a label to its referent does have support through reduced search (Ozcelik, Kalyuga, above). The closest analogue in prose is using one color to link a term to its definition or a claim to its evidence; that mechanism is plausible but untested in text.

**Grammar and part of speech.** The weakest layer. Evidence consists of a 2007 thesis (faster reading with POS-colored fonts), a 2026 preprint in non-Latin scripts (Khmer n=43: keyword memory 58.2% to 61.5%, factual questions about -5 points; Japanese n=10: about +8 s reading time, inference odds ratio 1.31), L2 gender and article dissertations, and small clinical studies where color is confounded with explicit instruction ([McConnell 2007](https://core.ac.uk/download/12209632.pdf), [Buoy et al. 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00688v1), [Kohler dissertation](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/handle/1808/10026), [Speech and Language UK on Colourful Semantics](https://speechandlanguage.org.uk/evidence-rating/moderate)). The textual-enhancement benchmark is small (d about 0.30 across 45 studies; 0.22 in another) versus about 0.93 for explicit rule presentation ([Lancaster summary](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/document/26274)). Read this as a learner scaffold with a legend, not a fluent-reader aid; the notes' inference, following Sarkar's pattern, is that benefit scales with how much the reader needs to attend to the coded dimension and shrinks with expertise.

**AI-marked meaning (main idea, claim, evidence, caveat, action).** The best rationale (signaling, instructor-quality highlighting, relation-cueing as in Richter's text-picture signaling) and the largest unknown. No study of algorithmic or LLM-chosen emphasis exists (notes, section 8). The notes' inference is that organizational cues (main ideas, relations) are more defensible than cues on arbitrary words, and that selection quality matters more than visual design.

## Channel, count and contrast numbers

**How many categories.** Cowan's capacity is about four chunks in the focus of attention ([Cowan 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/)), and up to seven isoluminant hues can be searched rapidly, but that is for squares on blank fields, not text ([Healey](https://healey.csc.ncsu.edu/publications/15828.pdf)). The notes treat 7 as an upper bound and about 4 as the learnable legend size; neither was tested on text. CVD-safe established sets top out at 7 to 9 (Okabe-Ito 8 including black; Tol bright 7, muted 9) ([Okabe and Ito](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/), [Tol](https://sronpersonalpages.nl/~pault/)). Tol also reports that the largest minimum contrast ratio between scheme colors shrinks as the set grows (2.8 for two colors, 2.1 for three, 1.5 for six), so more colors means smaller lightness steps between neighbors.

**Which cue types.** No head-to-head study ranks text color, background fill, bold, underline, italic or size on retention or reading speed (notes, section 7). The weak signals: bold cueing spared recall of uncued text while capitals did not (a 1977 study, authors unconfirmed), bold and italic lowered word recall relative to regular font in a 2015 adult study ([Ergonomics 1977](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140137708931601), [PubMed 26513175](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26513175/)), and italics were consistently worst for dyslexic readers ([Rello and Baeza-Yates 2013 via summary](https://blog.dyslexia.com/?p=872)). Redundant coding (color plus a second dimension) raised identification accuracy and cut latency in a NASA-cited summary, though a 1982 study found no effect and the amounts are unquantified ([NASA NTRS summary, secondary](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19920024935/downloads/19920024935.pdf)). WCAG 1.4.1 requires that color not be the only means, and counts a 3:1 lightness difference as an extra cue ([W3C 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)).

**Contrast and color vision.** WCAG 2.2 requires 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text (18 pt, or 14 pt bold) and for non-text indicators; AAA is 7:1 ([W3C 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html), [1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html), [1.4.6](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-enhanced.html)). Roughly 8% of men and 0.4 to 0.5% of women of Northern European ancestry have red-green CVD, lower in other populations, and blue-yellow and achromatopsia are about 1 in 10,000 or rarer, with sources disagreeing on tritan prevalence (0.001% to 0.008%) ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/color-vision-deficiency/), [Okabe and Ito](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/)). The notes' own WCAG-formula calculations, which should be re-verified in code, show that on white only Okabe-Ito blue (5.19) and a few Tol hues (4477AA 4.70, 228833 4.53, AA3377 6.09) pass 4.5:1 as text, while most Okabe-Ito hues pass on #121212 (blue falls to 3.61). Black text on saturated Okabe-Ito chips passes for every hue except blue (4.05), and Tol "light" hues give 8.3 to 15.5:1 with black text; Tol says his saturated schemes are too strong as backgrounds behind black text and points to the light and pale schemes for that use ([Tol](https://sronpersonalpages.nl/~pault/)). W3C notes protanopes may struggle with predominantly red text on dark backgrounds, and Okabe and Ito also advise avoiding red on black. The WCAG ratio is hue-blind and polarity-symmetric, and APCA critiques of it are advocacy-heavy and not a standard ([APCA author repo](https://github.com/Myndex/apca-introduction)), so treat 4.5:1 as a floor. In forced-colors mode the browser replaces author text, background, border and decoration colors and removes shadows and gradient backgrounds, so meaning carried only by fills disappears ([MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/forced-colors)).

**Consistency.** Consistent stimulus-response mappings can become automatic and varied ones do not (Schneider and Shiffrin 1977, with later critiques) ([summary](https://www.jimdavies.org/summaries/schneider1977.html)); semantically resonant colors sped chart reading ([Lin et al. 2013](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/semantically-resonant-colors)). The harm of remapping and the benefit of self-chosen versus designer-chosen codes were not found in any source.

## Design rules

Tier labels: **E** is directly supported by the cited evidence; **I** is inference from it, to be treated as a hypothesis.

| Topic | Rule | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Amount styled | Default to sparse: about one key sentence's worth of emphasis per paragraph or about five key statements per article for the meaning layer. Any percentage is your own experimental parameter. Expose it as a setting with a hard cap | E for the sparse protocols (Rickards and August; Cashen and Leicht via [Dunlosky](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)); I for the cap and any percent |
| Meaning-bearing colors | At most 4 at once, with a persistent legend; never more than the 6 to 8 hues of an established CVD-safe set | I (Cowan plus Healey, [Okabe and Ito](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/), Tol) |
| Which layers run together | Meaning layer plus structure on by default; content-type linking on demand; grammar layer off by default and shown only as a deliberate mode with a legend | E (weak, for grammar), I |
| Cue pairing | Every semantic color gets one non-color twin (underline style, border, tint, or marker); at most color plus one other cue per token | E for need ([WCAG 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)); I for stacking limit (no evidence) |
| Text color vs chip vs weight vs underline | No evidence ranks them. Prefer cues that do not change line breaks (underline style, border, tint, marker) over weight, italics, small caps or font changes. Use pale tint with dark text for chips, not saturated fills. Avoid capitals and italics in body text | I for layout-stability; E (weak) for caps/italics ([Ergonomics 1977](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140137708931601), [Rello](https://blog.dyslexia.com/?p=872)) |
| Colored glyphs | On light surfaces use darkened hues only where 4.5:1 is computed and met; otherwise use the saturated hue as an underline or border (3:1) with dark text. On dark surfaces check each hue against the actual surface (not pure black), and aim for 7:1 or more | E (WCAG); I (7:1 margin, per APCA critique and polarity studies) |
| Palette source | Start from Okabe-Ito (orange E69F00, sky blue 56B4E9, bluish green 009E73, yellow F0E442, blue 0072B2, vermillion D55E00, reddish purple CC79A7, black; black unretrieved in the notes) or Tol bright, muted or light, then derive per-surface variants and run deutan and protan simulation ([Machado et al. 2009](https://www.inf.ufrgs.br/~oliveira/gmmachado.html), [colorspace](https://colorspace.r-forge.r-project.org/articles/color_vision_deficiency.html)) | E (palettes), I (variants) |
| Consistency | Freeze the color-to-meaning table across documents and sessions; remapping is a deliberate, versioned user action. Keep it identical in light and dark modes (same cues, per-surface hues) | I (Schneider and Shiffrin, Lin et al.); harm of remapping is unmeasured |
| Synesthesia personalization | Build a calibration flow modeled on the Synesthesia Battery (pick a color per letter and digit, repeat, check consistency). Offer population tendencies only as labeled weak starting suggestions. Offer a "neutral glyph" mode: black text with colored underline or halo. Allow adjustable intensity | I (notes' own inferences from [Carmichael et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5047354)); benefit unstudied |
| Typography | Sans-serif or roman, no italics body, spacing controls rather than a "dyslexia font," dark-on-light default for small text with a dark-mode toggle for comfort, 55-75 characters per line as a harmless convention | E (moderate: [Marinus 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27194598/), [Zorzi 2012](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22665803/), [Piepenbrock 2013](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140139.2013.790485)) |
| Spacing | Increase letter spacing together with word spacing; honor user overrides of 1.5 line height, 2x paragraph, 0.12 letter and 0.16 word spacing (WCAG 1.4.12) | E ([Galliussi et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700), [W3C 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html)) |
| Accessibility modes | Honor `prefers-color-scheme`, `prefers-contrast` and `forced-colors`; under forced colors fall back to underline, border and marker semantics | E (platform behavior, [MDN](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/forced-colors)) |
| What NOT to do | No colored overlay or tint presented as a reading aid ([Griffiths 2016](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/104441/)); no Bionic-style start-bolding as a speed or comprehension feature (Parker, Snell, Beelders); no RSVP word-at-a-time reading (comprehension drops at higher speeds, [APS](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/speed-reading-apps-may-impair-reading-comprehension-by-limiting-ability-to-backtrack.html)); no BeeLine-style gradient claims (vendor data only); no dyslexia-font or Lexend claims; no hue-only semantics; no red text on black without a second cue; no dense auto-highlighting; no memory-gain or dyslexia-treatment claims on synesthesia grounds | E (negative) as cited |
| AI selection | Optimize selection precision before visuals; when the model is unsure, mark nothing. Let the reader accept, edit or dismiss marks | I ([Gier et al.](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/the-effects-of-pre-existing-inappropriate-highlighting-on-reading-comprehension/811806564355670018-25405), generation effect); hybrid untested |

## What is unstudied, stated bluntly

No peer-reviewed study tests coloured fonts, overlays or auto-styled text as an aid or hindrance for natural synesthetes, and the notes found no peer-reviewed experience reports. No study measures how much styling is optimal for reading, comprehension or retention; there is no dose-response data on percentage emphasized, on number of colors in prose, or on number of stacked cues. No head-to-head compares text color, background chip, weight, underline, italic or size on retention or reading speed. No study tests multi-category color coding of prose with delayed retention against single-color and no-color controls. No study tests algorithmic or LLM-chosen emphasis against human-chosen emphasis. Also missing: the time it takes to learn an arbitrary color code, the harm of remapping a learned code, whether self-chosen mappings are remembered better, and cultural variation in color meaning. For synesthesia specifically, nothing addresses projector versus associator differences as moderators of a tool's effect, or dyslexia in synesthetes (the notes found only a thesis proposal and a weak blog anecdote). The halation or astigmatism claim for dark mode has no controlled primary study in the notes. Finally, the notes' own limits matter: many sources were read at abstract level, several primary papers were blocked, and the arXiv preprint and vendor results are not peer reviewed.

## Prioritized changes to the tool

1. **Make the AI-meaning layer the primary layer and cap it.** Hard-limit marks per paragraph and per document (starting from one key sentence per paragraph), let the model abstain, and make the cap a user setting that is logged. Highest evidence-to-effort ratio.
2. **Demote the grammar layer to an optional, legend-bearing mode, off by default**, and stop styling every word of a reply or document by default. It has the weakest support and the strongest dilution risk.
3. **Cut meaning-bearing colors to about 4 and give each a non-color twin** (underline style, border, tint or marker) that does not change line breaks; never stack more than color plus one other cue.
4. **Add a per-surface contrast and CVD validator.** Compute WCAG ratios for every foreground/background pair in code (4.5:1 text, 3:1 large text and non-text, 7:1 target on dark), run deutan and protan simulation, and reject failing pairs. Use pale tints with dark text for chips.
5. **Freeze the color-to-meaning mapping and show a legend**; make remapping an explicit, versioned action, identical across light and dark.
6. **Build the synesthesia calibration and a "neutral glyph" mode** (black text with colored underline or halo). Add an "intensity" slider. Treat your own palette as untested until Experiment 5 below.
7. **Add spacing and polarity controls** (letter plus word plus line spacing, dark-on-light default, dark-mode toggle, sans-serif, no italics, no capitals for emphasis) and honor `prefers-contrast` and `forced-colors`.
8. **Add per-layer toggles and built-in logging** (which layers, how much styled, reading time) so the experiments below cost almost nothing.
9. **Add an optional retrieval step** (a recall question after reading), since practice testing and spacing outrank highlighting in Dunlosky's ratings, and remove every performance claim from the interface.
10. **Do not build** Bionic bolding, RSVP, gradient lines, overlay presets or a dyslexia font.

## Low-cost experiments to run on yourself

Single-person results are noisy: the Readwise Bionic data split 52% faster with and 48% faster without, which is what noise around zero looks like ([Readwise](https://blog.readwise.io/bionic-reading-results/), vendor/practitioner source). You cannot blind yourself, so use many short passages, randomize condition order, write the questions before you see the styling, and log how you felt alongside the score, since perceived and measured benefit can diverge ([PMC9674548](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9674548/), weak).

1. **Layers on versus off.** Same-difficulty passages of similar length, alternated randomly between plain and styled. Record words per minute, then a short comprehension quiz immediately and the same quiz a week later (delayed retention is where the literature is thinnest).
2. **Density dose-response.** Same passage family at four levels: none, sparse (one key sentence per paragraph), medium, heavy. Compare delayed recall of marked and unmarked content separately, to see whether unmarked content pays a cost.
3. **Channel comparison.** Same meaning layer rendered as text color, pale tint chip, underline, or bold, then run a skim task (find all items of type X) and a delayed recall test, to separate finding speed from retention.
4. **Category count.** Two, four or six meaning categories, with and without a legend; test recognition of what each color meant a week later.
5. **Synesthesia clash test.** Read with the tool's colors, with your own letter-color palette in the neutral-glyph mode, and with no color; rate comfort and measure speed. For a sharper test, run a colour-naming task with words in tool colors congruent versus incongruent to your own letter colors, which mirrors the Stroop paradigm that is known to produce interference ([van Leeuwen et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2919410)).
6. **Selection quality.** Have the AI mark a document, then mark your own key sentences blind to its picks, and compare recall of AI-marked, self-marked and unmarked content. This is the only test of the tool's central bet and of the Gier-type harm from wrong marks.
7. **Retrieval versus styling.** Re-read styled text versus write answers from memory, tested a week later, to check Dunlosky's ranking on your own data.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the tool: color is not a memory device, it is an attention device whose value depends on where it points. The synesthesia literature adds no design lever beyond personalization, since your memory advantage, if you have it, is general, and the one confirmed interference effect concerns naming, not reading. The result is that the right design is a restrained, accurate, accessible emphasis system with an honest legend, and the valuable work is measuring it.

The notes' deepest gap is also your opportunity. Because there is no dose-response, channel, category-count or AI-selection data for anyone, let alone for a grapheme-color synesthete, a tool that logs its own styling and your reading performance generates the missing evidence. Prefer small, falsifiable settings changes tested on yourself over adding layers, and treat any benefit you find as personal rather than general.
