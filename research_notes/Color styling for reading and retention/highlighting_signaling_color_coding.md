# Highlighting, color-coding and signaling in text and multimedia learning: evidence for an automatic text-styling tool

Evidence labels used throughout: **[strong]** = multiple converging meta-analyses or large replicated literatures; **[moderate]** = one meta-analysis or several consistent primary studies; **[weak]** = single or small studies, abstract-level only, or indirect; **[none]** = searched for and not found, or tested with null results. Effect sizes are as reported by the sources; whether g or d is stated is noted where known. Many sources could only be read at abstract or secondary-summary level in this session (flagged "abstract-level"). Dunlosky et al. 2013 was read in full text.

## 1. Dunlosky et al. 2013 and the highlighting/underlining literature: why low utility, and when it helps

### Takeaway
Dunlosky et al. rated highlighting/underlining "low utility" (alongside summarization, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading) because, as students typically use it (self-generated, unselective), it usually gives no benefit over simply reading, and may hurt inference. The benefit appears when marking is sparse, accurate (often expert/instructor-chosen), and the test targets the marked facts. The 2022 meta-analysis (Ponce, Mayer, Méndez) quantifies this: instructor-provided highlighting about g/d 0.44 on memory and comprehension; learner-generated 0.36 on memory but only 0.20 (non-significant) on comprehension.

### Cited Findings
- Dunlosky, Rawson, Marsh, Nathan and Willingham (2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest 14(1), 4-58) rated practice testing and distributed practice HIGH; elaborative interrogation, self-explanation and interleaved practice MODERATE; summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery use for text and rereading LOW. [strong; full text read] — [Dunlosky et al. 2013 PDF](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Dunlosky's Table 4 generalizability ratings for highlighting: learners = Qualified, materials = Qualified, criterion tasks = Negative ("largely ineffective"), issues for implementation = Positive, educational contexts = Negative. (Qualified = positive under some conditions only.) — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Dunlosky's text: "most studies have shown no benefit of highlighting (as it is typically used) over and above the benefit of simply reading"; lack of benefit on immediate and delayed tests (1 week to 1 month) and across free recall, factual MC, comprehension MC, sentence completion. — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Fowler and Barker (1974, Exp. 1): undergraduates read ~8,000 words of Scientific American/Science articles; control vs active highlighters vs passive (yoked) readers of marked text; 1 hour study, 1 week later 10-min review then 54-item MC test. Highlighting groups did NOT outperform control overall. Within active highlighters, items on highlighted text were better answered; that benefit was larger for active than passive highlighters, and came with a small cost on non-highlighted material. Test score correlated r = -.29 with amount highlighted (n = 19, non-significant). — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Mechanism in Dunlosky: the isolation (von Restorff) effect, i.e., marked text "pops out"; overmarking reduces distinctiveness (Lorch, Lorch & Klusewitz 1995) and takes less processing than selecting few key details. — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Constraining the amount helps: Rickards and August (1975) one underlined sentence per paragraph gave better recall of a science text than no underlining; L. L. Johnson (1988) one sentence per paragraph helped memory for the underlined information but not overall. Glover et al. (1980) trained underlining with feedback and penalties for extraneous underlining. Two of three training studies improved performance (Amer 1994; Hayati and Shariatifar 2009). — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Experimenter/instructor marking: Cashen and Leicht (1970) underlined 5 critical statements per Scientific American article in red; exam items on those statements were better, and items from adjacent sentences were also better (no cost); replicated more weakly (Leicht and Cashen 1972). Dunlosky cautions this may not generalize to learner-controlled marking, since learners mark far more than five statements. — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Student characteristics: highlighting failed to help Air Force trainees, children (Rickards and Denner 1979), remedial students (mean SAT-V 390; Nist and Hogrebe 1987) and prototypical undergraduates (Todd and Kessler 1971). Premarked text helped airmen high in mechanical aptitude more (Klare et al. 1955), suggesting prior knowledge moderates. A $5 prize for top scorers produced an underlining benefit (Fass and Schumacher 1978), suggesting students can highlight well but often do not. Underlining helped more with a difficult text than an easy one (Fass and Schumacher 1978). — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Possible harm: Peterson (1992), education majors, 10,000-word history chapter, 90 min study; underliners equal on factual questions but worse on inference questions at 1 week and 2 months. Dunlosky flags this as needing replication. Underliners also tune to the expected test format (Kulhavy et al. 1975). — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Dunlosky's bottom line: highlighting "does little to boost performance," may help when students have the knowledge to highlight well or texts are difficult, but may hurt higher-level (inference) tasks. Bell and Limber (cited as 2010 in Dunlosky; indexed elsewhere as 2009) is cited only for prevalence of marking in textbooks. — [Dunlosky et al. 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf); [Bell and Limber record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/the-role-of-underlining-and-annotating-in-remembering-textual-information/811807137725415425-25405) (abstract-level: survey of intro-psych students' marking; findings not retrieved)
- Ponce, Mayer and Méndez (2022, Educational Psychology Review 34, 989-1024): meta-analysis of 36 articles (1938-2019), 85 effect sizes, college and K-12. Learner-generated highlighting: memory 0.36, comprehension 0.20 (not significant); college 0.39, school students 0.24 (no benefit). Instructor-provided: 0.44 for both memory and comprehension; college 0.41, school 0.48. [moderate; abstract-level; effect-size type and N not retrieved] — [Ponce et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09654-1)
- Secondary report of the same meta-analysis: students trained in marking beat read-only students, g = 1.02 (p = .002); the 0.36 memory effect has p = .013 and the 0.20 comprehension effect p = .155. [weak, secondary] — [Frontiers in Education 2025, 140 undergraduates on text-marking](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1510007/full)
- Same Frontiers 2025 study: 140 undergraduates, 2x2 (example training x recall/transfer orientation); marking amount predicted transfer (partial eta-sq = .042) but training/orientation did not change posttest scores; prior knowledge mediated ~52% of the marking-achievement link. [weak] — [Frontiers 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1510007/full)
- Yue, Storm, Kornell and Bjork (2015, Educational Psychology Review 27, 69-78): text read twice, highlighting vs not, massed vs distributed reading, test a week later. Highlighting helped overall, most under massed reading; no impairment of non-highlighted information; students skeptical of highlighting benefited MORE than pro-highlighting students. [moderate-weak; abstract-level; N not retrieved] — [Yue et al. record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/highlighting-and-its-relation-to-distributed-study-and-students-metacognitive-beliefs/814713310267047937-10463)
- Ben-Yehudah and Eshet-Alkalai (2018, J. Educational Multimedia and Hypermedia 27(2)): college-level expository text, print vs digital, between-subjects. Digital readers did worse without highlighting; highlighting helped only the print group, improved inferential but not literal questions; authors conclude highlighting may be a weak tool for digital learning. [weak-moderate; abstract-level; N not retrieved] — [Ben-Yehudah and Eshet-Alkalai 2018](https://cris.openu.ac.il/en/publications/the-contribution-of-text-highlighting-to-comprehension-a-comparis/)
- Gier and colleagues (2010 Reading Psychology; 2011 J. College Reading and Learning) and Silvers and Kreiner (1997): pre-existing INAPPROPRIATE highlighting (someone else's, on the wrong passages) lowered reading comprehension and metacomprehension; appropriate highlighting neither helped nor hurt retention (Silvers and Kreiner); using a finger or a blue highlighter, or an electronic highlighter, removed the harm. [moderate-weak; abstract-level] — [Gier et al. 2011 record](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/the-effects-of-pre-existing-inappropriate-highlighting-on-reading-comprehension/811806564355670018-25405); [Gier et al. 2010](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/02702710903397082)
- Dual-color highlighting (two colors for two idea types) neither improved nor reduced comprehension vs single-color highlighting; highlighted info was better comprehended regardless of method (Metzger and Fuson-Newsome 2016 as described by the search summary). [weak; small undergraduate-journal source] — [JUROS OSU](https://juros.osu.edu/article/id/4399)

### Inferences
- Highlighting is rated low largely because of HOW learners use it (too much, wrong things), not because emphasis itself is inert. The conditions that produce benefit (few marks, correct targets, expert selection) are exactly what an automatic styling tool can control, so Dunlosky's low rating is weak evidence against tool-imposed emphasis. However, the benefit that survives is mostly "memory for the marked material," a narrower claim than comprehension.
- Dunlosky's own highest-rated techniques (practice testing, distributed practice) are not emphasis techniques; a styling tool should not be presented as a substitute for them.
- Benefit/harm asymmetry: marked content gains, unmarked content often shows a small cost (Fowler and Barker) or no cost (Cashen and Leicht, Yue et al.). Mixed.

### Gaps
- Sample sizes and exact designs for Yue 2015, Ben-Yehudah 2018, Gier 2010/2011 and Bell and Limber were not retrieved. The Ponce 2022 full text (heterogeneity, text-type moderators, effect-size type) was not read.
- Bell and Limber's actual findings on marking and course performance were not found.

## 2. Signaling (cueing) principle: meta-analyses, effect sizes, moderators, "too many cues"

### Takeaway
Three meta-analyses agree signaling helps, with small-to-moderate effects: Schneider et al. 2018 g+ = 0.53 retention, 0.33 transfer (103 studies, N = 12,201); Alpizar et al. 2020 d = .38 (29 studies, 2,726); Richter et al. 2016 r = .17 for text-picture signaling (27 studies, 2,464). Prior knowledge as a moderator is disputed. No located source quantifies a "too many cues" penalty directly.

### Cited Findings
- Schneider, Beege, Nebel and Rey (2018, Educational Research Review 23, 1-24): 103 studies, N = 12,201; retention g+ = 0.53 (95% CI .42-.64), transfer g+ = 0.33; also reduced perceived difficulty/effort. Nine moderators tested incl. type of signaling; prior knowledge was NOT identified as a moderator (contrary to expertise-reversal expectation). [strong-moderate; abstract-level via secondary summaries] — [ScienceDirect record](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X17300581); [summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/signalling)
- Richter, Scheiter and Eitel (2016, Educational Research Review 17, 19-36): signaling TEXT-PICTURE relations; 27 studies, 45 comparisons, N = 2,464; overall r = .17 (95% CI .11-.22) for transfer/comprehension, small-to-medium; benefit strongest for LOW prior knowledge learners (expertise-reversal pattern); moderators coded: prior knowledge, pacing, pictorial format, mapping requirements, signal distinctiveness. [strong-moderate; abstract-level] — [Richter et al. 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X15000664); [data record](https://psycharchives.org/handle/20.500.12034/2019)
- Alpizar, Adesope and Wong (2020, Educational Technology Research and Development 68(5), 2095-2119): 29 studies, 44 effect sizes, N = 2,726; d = .38; stronger in high-quality studies, studies with pretests, reliability reported, prior knowledge controlled. [moderate; abstract-level] — [Alpizar et al. 2020](https://bohrium.dp.tech/paper/arxiv/812517839771533313)
- Mayer's Cambridge Handbook chapter reports signaling supported in 3 of 3 experimental tests, median effect size 0.60 (older, uncorrected for publication bias). The later meta-analyses give smaller pooled estimates. [moderate; secondary] — [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-reducing-extraneous-processing-in-multimedia-learning-coherence-signaling-redundancy-spatial-contiguity-and-temporal-contiguity-principles/C98AB3A6CE760DD63C048936EA0B3B44)
- Prior knowledge findings conflict: Richter 2016 reports benefit mainly for low-prior-knowledge learners; Schneider 2018 reports no prior-knowledge moderation. — [Richter](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1747938X15000664) vs [Schneider summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/signalling)
- Lorch, Lorch and Klusewitz (1995): effects of typographical cues on recall are mediated by attention during reading; Dunlosky cites it for overmarking reducing distinctiveness. — [Lorch et al. 1995](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/effects-of-typographical-cues-on-reading-and-recall-of-text/); [Dunlosky 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)

### Inferences
- Pooled signaling effects (about 0.2-0.5) are of the same order as instructor-provided highlighting (0.44), consistent across literatures. Both meta-analyses are dominated by multimedia/instructional-design materials; much is text+picture or narrated animation, not plain prose, so transfer to pure text styling is partly by analogy.
- Because signaling works by guiding attention to organizationally relevant content, a tool that cues organizational structure (main ideas, relations) should be more defensible than one that cues arbitrary words.
- "Too many cues hurt" is plausible from isolation-effect logic and Dunlosky's overmarking point, but I found no signaling meta-analytic dose-response estimate.

### Gaps
- Full text of Schneider 2018 and Richter 2016 (signal-type moderator values, text-only vs text-picture breakdown, amount-of-signaling results) could not be accessed (403 / unreadable PDFs). The van Gog handbook chapter was blocked (403). A PLOS ONE 2017 meta-analysis titled "The More Total Cognitive Load is Reduced by Cues, the Better Retention and Transfer of Multimedia Learning" surfaced only as a title in a reference list; unverified.
- No direct dose-response evidence on number of cues was located.

## 3. Color coding specifically: text+picture vs text-only

### Takeaway
Color coding helps when it links text labels to diagram parts (reduces visual search): positive, moderate evidence from small lab studies. For color-coding categories within prose, I found no study showing a recall benefit over a no-color control, and some null results.

### Cited Findings
- Kalyuga, Chandler and Sweller (1999, Applied Cognitive Psychology 13, 351-371): Experiment 2 showed color coding reduced search for diagram referents in the text and eased split-attention. (Experiment 1: auditory text reduced split attention.) Participants and effect sizes not retrieved. [weak-moderate] — [Kalyuga et al. 1999](https://tecfa.unige.ch/tecfa/teaching/methodo/Kalyuga99.pdf)
- Ozcelik, Karakus, Kursun and Cagiltay (2009, Computers and Education 53(2), 445-453): 52 participants, color-coded vs conventional multimedia lesson; color coding improved retention and transfer; eye tracking (Tobii 1750) attributed the gain to faster location of corresponding items in illustration and text and attention to salient information. [moderate-weak; abstract-level; effect sizes not retrieved] — [Ozcelik et al. 2009](https://open.metu.edu.tr/handle/11511/28595)
- Ozcelik, Arslan-Ari and Cagiltay (2010, Computers in Human Behavior 26(1), 110-117): 40 undergraduates; signaling = temporarily changing color of labels in the illustration; signaled group better on transfer and matching tests; eye movements showed less visual search and longer fixation on relevant labels/areas. [weak-moderate; small N] — [Ozcelik et al. 2010](https://ada.atilim.edu.tr/entities/publication/d5afcf81-dea9-449d-85e8-b65df9e039c3)
- Text-only categories: dual-color highlighting did not beat single-color (see Section 1). A seventh-grade study (n = 132) found the fit between graphic/color presentation and the assessment type mattered more than the presence of color coding at immediate recall. [weak; thesis-level] — [JUROS OSU](https://juros.osu.edu/article/id/4399); [UNI thesis](https://scholarworks.uni.edu/etd/834)
- Lorch et al. cite attention-mediation; a search summary found no isolation of semantic color coding of expository text against a no-color control. [none] 

### Inferences
- Color's demonstrated value is as a MAPPING aid (same color on the label and on its referent), i.e., reducing search cost. That mechanism does not apply to prose without a paired referent unless the tool creates one (e.g., color links a term to its definition, or a heading to its section).
- In text-only settings, a color-category scheme asks readers to learn a legend; no evidence shows this yields recall gains, and learning the key adds load.

### Gaps
- No primary study located that tests multi-category color coding of prose (e.g., claims/evidence/examples) against single-color and no-color controls with delayed retention. "Technical-prose color cueing showed no retention difference" appeared in one search summary without an identifiable source; unverified.
- Effect sizes (d/g) for Ozcelik 2009/2010 and Kalyuga 1999 not retrieved.

## 4. The von Restorff (isolation) effect and how many items to style

### Takeaway
Distinctive items are better remembered; Dunlosky explicitly invokes this as the mechanism for highlighting, and warns that overmarking destroys distinctiveness. Strong theoretical support; no study located that gives a quantitative "optimal proportion" for text styling.

### Cited Findings
- Isolation effect described by Dunlosky as a semantically/perceptually unique item remembered better, with the analogy that highlighted/underlined/capitalized sentences "pop out"; overmarking reduces distinctiveness. [strong as principle] — [Dunlosky 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Hunt (1995) argues modern theory stresses perceptual salience and differential attention; Hunt and Lamb (2001) argue instead for processing of similarities and differences (distinctiveness is "processing of difference in the context of similarity," Hunt 2006); the effect can arise even when the isolate comes first. [moderate; abstract-level] — [Hunt and Lamb 2001 record](https://pesquisa.bvsalud.org/brasil/resource/en/mdl-11713872); [Huff, Bodner and Fawcett 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4305508)
- Meta-analysis of distinctive encoding (DRM paradigm): distinctive encoding raised correct list-item memory (g = 0.82 between-subjects); within-subject designs showed a "distinctiveness cost" for already-distinctive lists (g = -0.19) and benefit for non-distinctive lists (g = 0.50). Note: false-memory paradigm, not reading comprehension. [moderate for word lists; indirect for prose] — [Huff et al. 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4305508)
- Typography: bold cueing did not lower recall of uncued material, whereas capital-letter cueing did (1977 Ergonomics study, author not confirmed); in younger and older adults, regular font gave higher recall than italic or bold items in a word-recall task. [weak] — [Ergonomics 1977 record](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140137708931601); [PubMed 26513175](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26513175/)
- Quantity limits: Rickards and August (one sentence per paragraph), L. L. Johnson (1988), Cashen and Leicht (5 statements per article) all show benefit under sparse marking. — [Dunlosky 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)

### Inferences
- Distinctiveness is relative to context; each additional style competes with the others. Styling many items converts the "isolate" into the background. Sparse, rule-bound marking (on the order of one key sentence per paragraph in the studies above) has the best support; I would treat any specific percentage as a design hypothesis, not a finding.
- Different style channels (hue vs weight) may allow a few distinct "isolates" without mutual dilution, but I found no evidence on that.

### Gaps
- Direct tests of how the proportion of styled text affects recall (dose-response) were not found. Hunt and Lamb's full text on proportion/rarity could not be read. Original von Restorff (1933) and Hunt and Worthen 2006 not accessed.

## 5. Working memory limits and number of usable colors

### Takeaway
Cowan's ~4-chunk estimate and Healey's finding that up to 7 isoluminant colors can be searched rapidly give a rough ceiling: about 4 categories to hold in mind, about 5-7 at most to distinguish perceptually. These are different limits and neither is a direct text-styling result.

### Cited Findings
- Cowan (2001, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 24, 87-114): capacity of about four chunks in the focus of attention, argued as a replacement for Miller's "seven"; disputed by some researchers. [strong-moderate] — [Cowan 2001 PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11515286/)
- Healey (talk on perceptual colors for visualization): "up to seven isoluminant colors can be displayed simultaneously" while allowing rapid, accurate identification of any one, with response time independent of display size (preattentive); required controlling color distance, linear separation and named color category together; targets were harder when target and distractors fell in the same named color region (Kawai et al.). [moderate; lab, adults, squares not text] — [Healey](https://healey.csc.ncsu.edu/publications/15828.pdf)
- Healey and Enns (2012, IEEE TVCG 18(7), 1170-1188) review: preattentive features (hue among them) are found independent of distractor count; conjunction targets cannot be found preattentively; hue-on-form interference hierarchy shown in figures. — [Healey and Enns 2012 PDF](https://visionlab-psych.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2015/06/140_HealeyEnns_IEEE2011.pdf); [record](https://healey.csc.ncsu.edu/publications/15783)
- Healey talk: hue dominates form in boundary detection (random hue masks form boundaries; random form does not mask hue). — [Healey](https://healey.csc.ncsu.edu/publications/15828.pdf)

### Inferences
- Perceptual discriminability (~7 hues under ideal conditions) is not the same as semantic memory for a legend (~4). A tool using more than about 4 meaning-bearing colors likely taxes readers; this is inference from Cowan plus Healey, not tested on text.
- Text is not an isoluminant square on a blank field: thin glyphs, small color areas, and background-color highlights reduce effective discriminability, so Healey's 7 should be treated as an upper bound for text.

### Gaps
- "Miller 7+-2 revisions", Ware's color guidance and the "5 to 7 colors" rule of thumb were not independently sourced in this session; categorical color perception (Kawai) is covered only through Healey's summary.

## 6. Dual coding theory (Paivio): color as a second code?

### Takeaway
Nothing retrieved in this session supports color as a "second code" for verbal material. Dual coding concerns verbal vs imaginal systems; color is a perceptual attribute, not an image.

### Cited Findings
- Dunlosky notes ongoing, unresolved debates about whether imagery's power comes from dual codes or a distinctive propositional code (Marschark and Hunt 1989). [weak] — [Dunlosky 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Observed color benefits (Section 3) were explained by attention guidance and search efficiency (eye tracking), not by a dual code. — [Ozcelik et al. 2010](https://ada.atilim.edu.tr/entities/publication/d5afcf81-dea9-449d-85e8-b65df9e039c3)

### Inferences
- Frame color as an attention/organization cue (signaling), not a dual-code memory trace.

### Gaps
- No direct sources on Paivio's theory or on color as a verbal-memory code were retrieved. [none found]

## 7. Colored text vs colored background vs bold/underline/italic/size; scanning and skimming

### Takeaway
Head-to-head evidence in reading is thin: no retrieved study ranks highlight background vs text color vs bold on retention. The visual-search literature says hue is a strong preattentive feature, but conjunctions and interference reduce its power.

### Cited Findings
- Hue (and orientation, size, etc.) are preattentive: a unique-hue target is found at near-constant time regardless of distractor number; conjunction targets are not (Treisman-style findings as reviewed by Healey and Enns). [strong for lab displays] — [Healey and Enns 2012](https://visionlab-psych.sites.olt.ubc.ca/files/2015/06/140_HealeyEnns_IEEE2011.pdf)
- Feature interference: random hue disrupts form-boundary detection but not vice versa. — [Healey](https://healey.csc.ncsu.edu/publications/15828.pdf)
- Bold vs capital letters: bold cueing spared recall of uncued text whereas capitals did not (1977 Ergonomics, authors not confirmed); bold and italic reduced word recall relative to regular font in younger/older adults (2015). [weak] — [Ergonomics record](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140137708931601); [PubMed 26513175](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26513175/)
- Lorch, Lorch and Klusewitz (1995): typographical cues affect memory via attention allocation during reading. [moderate; abstract-level] — [Lorch et al. 1995](https://scholars.uky.edu/en/publications/effects-of-typographical-cues-on-reading-and-recall-of-text/)
- Blue highlighter vs finger vs electronic highlighter all neutralised the harm of inappropriate prior highlighting (Gier et al.) — color of highlighter did not matter for that effect. [weak] — [Gier et al. 2010](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/full/10.1080/02702710903397082)

### Inferences
- Skimming is where preattentive color should help (finding marked items quickly), but skimming benefit is not the same as retention benefit.
- Text color vs background fill: not compared in the literature I found; legibility/contrast and accessibility should drive that choice.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head retention, reading-speed, or skim studies of background highlight vs text color vs bold/underline/italic/size were found. Treisman, Wolfe and Wolfe-style search primary sources were accessed only through Healey and Enns's review. WCAG/color-blindness is outside the searched evidence.

## 8. Machine-chosen vs learner-generated emphasis (generation effect)

### Takeaway
Generation helps in general (d about 0.40), but for highlighting, expert/instructor-chosen marking is at least as effective as learner-chosen and in meta-analysis more effective for comprehension. No study of algorithmically chosen highlighting was found.

### Cited Findings
- Generation effect meta-analysis (Bertsch, Pesta, Wiscott and McDaniel 2007): 86 studies, 445 effect sizes, overall effect .40; larger with longer retention intervals (secondary report). [strong for word-level tasks] — [Bertsch et al. 2007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17645161/)
- Fowler and Barker: active highlighters gained more on highlighted items than passive readers of the same marks; but Nist and Hogrebe (1987) found active marking not always better than receiving experimenter-highlighted text, "probably because experimenters will usually be better than students at highlighting the most important parts." — [Dunlosky 2013](https://www.wku.edu/senate/documents/improving_student_learning_dunlosky_2013.pdf)
- Ponce 2022: instructor-provided 0.44 (memory and comprehension) vs learner-generated 0.36 memory, 0.20 comprehension (n.s.); school students gained from instructor-provided (0.48) but not from learner-generated (0.24). — [Ponce et al. 2022](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10648-021-09654-1)
- Gier et al.: others' inappropriate highlighting harms; so poor-quality external highlighting is a real risk. — [Gier et al. 2011](https://www.bohrium.com/paper-details/the-effects-of-pre-existing-inappropriate-highlighting-on-reading-comprehension/811806564355670018-25405)

### Inferences
- If selection quality is high, tool-provided emphasis plausibly captures most of the instructor-provided benefit, especially for younger or lower-knowledge readers. If quality is low (generic keyword extraction), the Gier/Silvers-Kreiner results suggest it can hurt comprehension and mislead metacomprehension.
- The generation benefit comes from the reader's decision-making (organizational processing), which a passive styled text removes; a hybrid (tool suggests, reader confirms/edits) could preserve it but is untested.

### Gaps
- No study of automatic/algorithmic/LLM-generated highlighting versus human-chosen highlighting and learning outcomes was found in this session. Whether tool-selected emphasis plus learner action preserves generation benefits is unknown.

## Implications for a text-styling tool

Evidence statements (E) are separated from inferences (I).

- **E [moderate]:** Instructor/expert-provided highlighting improves memory and comprehension (~0.44 across college and school learners; Ponce et al. 2022, abstract-level). Learner-generated gives ~0.36 memory, ~0.20 comprehension (n.s.). Tool-provided emphasis is in the first category only if selection quality approximates an expert's.
- **I:** Optimise for selection quality (main ideas, relations) before visual design; poor selection is the failure mode (Gier et al.; Silvers and Kreiner [moderate-weak]).
- **E [strong as mechanism, moderate as dose-response]:** Marked content is better remembered (isolation effect) and over-marking erodes the effect; sparse constraints (e.g., one sentence per paragraph) show benefit (Dunlosky 2013 reviewing Rickards and August; L. L. Johnson; Cashen and Leicht).
- **I:** Cap styled text severely and make the cap a user setting; I found no evidence for a specific percentage, so treat any default as a hypothesis to test.
- **E [moderate-weak]:** Benefit is mainly for the cued content (small or no cost for uncued content). Inference-level comprehension can be unaffected or worsened by underlining (Peterson 1992 [weak, single study]; Ben-Yehudah 2018 found the reverse in print: inferential gain).
- **I:** Style relations/structure as well as isolated facts to avoid fragmenting comprehension; mixed evidence, so do not claim comprehension gains.
- **E [moderate]:** Signaling meta-analyses: retention g+ 0.53, transfer g+ 0.33 (Schneider 2018); d = .38 (Alpizar 2020); r = .17 (Richter 2016, text-picture).
- **I:** Expect small-to-moderate gains in the best case; claims in product copy should be modest. These meta-analyses are mainly multimedia, so pure-text transfer is inference.
- **E [moderate-weak]:** Color helps where it MAPS labels to referents (Kalyuga 1999; Ozcelik 2009, 2010), through reduced search; effect attributed to attention, per eye tracking.
- **I:** Use color to link related text elements (term to definition, claim to evidence) rather than as free decoration; this is the closest analogue to the supported mechanism.
- **E [none/weak]:** No evidence that coloring prose categories improves recall over single-color or no-color (dual-color highlighting null; Metzger-type study, thesis-level).
- **I:** A multi-hue semantic scheme for prose is unsupported; default to one emphasis channel and add categories only as an optional, testable mode.
- **E [moderate-weak]:** Working-memory ceiling about 4 chunks (Cowan 2001); about 7 isoluminant hues can be visually searched (Healey, squares on blank fields).
- **I:** Use at most about 3-4 meaning-bearing styles with a persistent, visible legend; more than 5-7 hues is beyond even ideal perceptual limits. Text rendering lowers the real limit.
- **E [none]:** Color as a "second code" (dual coding) for verbal material: no support found.
- **I:** Do not market color as dual coding.
- **E [strong for lab displays, indirect for reading]:** Hue is preattentive, and conjunctions (hue + other cue) are not (Healey and Enns 2012).
- **I:** For skim/scan use, one salient unique hue per target class supports fast finding; stacking multiple cues on the same item adds little and complicates search. Benefit for skimming does not establish retention benefit.
- **E [none]:** No head-to-head data on background fill vs text color vs bold/underline/italic/size for retention or reading speed. Weak data: bold spared uncued text where capitals did not (1977); bold/italic lowered word recall vs regular font in one 2015 adult study.
- **I:** Prefer bold or background tint over capitals; choose among channels on legibility/accessibility grounds and A/B-test retention.
- **E [moderate, indirect]:** Generation effect d about 0.40 in general (Bertsch 2007), but for highlighting experimenter marks match or beat self-marking (Nist and Hogrebe; Ponce 2022).
- **I:** Offer "accept/edit suggested emphasis" for engagement, not as a proven learning booster; keep reader control (users skeptical of highlighting benefited more in Yue et al. 2015 [weak]).
- **E [moderate]:** Highlighting is weaker than practice testing and distributed practice (Dunlosky 2013, strong).
- **I:** Position styling as a reading aid and pair with retrieval/spacing features if retention is the goal.
- **E [weak]:** Digital screens showed less highlighting benefit than print (Ben-Yehudah and Eshet-Alkalai 2018, single study).
- **I:** Validate on-screen effects empirically; do not assume transfer from print-based literature.
- **Population caution [moderate-weak]:** Learner-generated highlighting did not help school students in meta-analysis, while instructor-provided did; prior-knowledge moderation is mixed (Richter yes, Schneider no).
