**Evidence labels used throughout:** [STRONG] = normative standard, or multiple independent primary sources agree. [MODERATE] = at least one peer-reviewed primary study or authoritative documentation, replication or sample size not verified. [WEAK] = single or secondary source, advocacy source, or mechanism asserted but not verified. [NONE FOUND] = searched and found no usable evidence. "Unretrieved" means I could only see an abstract, snippet or search-tool summary, not the full text. Sample sizes are given only where I actually saw them.

**Research-method caveat:** Several primary pages blocked or garbled fetching (PubMed cookie wall, core.ac.uk 403, a scanned NASA PDF, personal.sron.nl DNS failure; Tol's page was read via its mirror). ## 1. Color vision deficiency (CVD) prevalence by type, sex and population

### Takeaway
About 8% of men and 0.4 to 0.5% of women of Northern European ancestry have red-green CVD [STRONG]. Prevalence is lower in Asian and especially African populations, and blue-yellow (tritan) deficiency and achromatopsia are rare (roughly 1 in 10,000 or rarer) [MODERATE]. Per-type splits (protan vs deutan) come from an unsourced table and from small regional studies, so treat them as approximate.

### Cited Findings
- Red-green CVD affects about 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women in Northern European-ancestry populations; overall CVD is estimated at about 2.5% of people worldwide; red-green is also common in East Asian populations and less common in almost all other studied populations; blue-yellow occurs in fewer than 1 in 10,000 people and affects men and women equally; red-green types and blue cone monochromacy are X-linked [STRONG for the NE figure, MODERATE for the rest] — [MedlinePlus Genetics](https://medlineplus.gov/genetics/condition/color-vision-deficiency/)
- Wong (2011, Nature Methods 8:441) states as many as 8% of men and 0.5% of women of Northern European ancestry have the common form of red-green color blindness [STRONG; reported via a search-tool summary of the column, full text not fetched] — [Wong 2011 bibliographic record](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/wong-pointsofviewcolorblindness)
- Slightly different female figure: a 2019 paper citing Birch (2012) gives approximately 8% in men and 0.4% in women for European Caucasians, and notes rising prevalence in men of African ethnicity — [Durban school-children study, PMC6558866](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6558866/). Sources disagree between 0.4% and 0.5% for women; either is "about 1 in 200".
- Okabe and Ito's page: "One in twelve Caucasian (8%), one in 20 Asian (5%), and one in 25 African (4%) males are so-called red-green colorblind"; blue-yellow (tritan) "only 0.001% of occurrence"; for a facility used by 10,000 people (50% male) they estimate 400 colorblind [MODERATE; page is an expert design guide, not a survey] — [Okabe and Ito, jfly.uni-koeln.de/color](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/). Note the tritan figure (0.001%) is ten times lower than MedlinePlus's "fewer than 1 in 10,000" (0.01%) and Wikipedia's 0.008%; the sources conflict.
- Birch (2012), J Opt Soc Am A 29(3):313-20, is a literature review of inherited red-green deficiency; abstract-level findings: the male-to-female prevalence ratio differs markedly between Europeans and Asians, and prevalence appears to be rising in men of African ethnicity and in areas settled by migrants [STRONG that the review exists and its qualitative conclusions; country-level numbers unretrieved] — [PubMed 22472762](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22472762/) (via search summary)
- Per-type table reproduced on Wikipedia (secondary, marked "citation needed" on the page): males, protanopia 1.3%, deuteranopia 1.2%, tritanopia 0.008%, protanomaly 1.3%, deuteranomaly 5.0%; females, protanopia 0.02%, deuteranopia 0.01%, tritanopia 0.008%, protanomaly 0.02%, deuteranomaly 0.35%; total dichromacy 2.4% (M) and 0.03% (F); total anomalous trichromacy 6.3% (M) and 0.37% (F); achromatopsia less than 0.0001%. The same page also shows a different set of figures in a chart caption (deuteranomaly 2.7%, protanomaly 0.66%, protanopia 0.59%, deuteranopia 0.56%), so per-type numbers are inconsistent [WEAK] — [Wikipedia: Color blindness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_blindness)
- Regional school surveys (all Ishihara-based unless noted), male / female %: Durban Black South African children 4.2 / 0.6 (n = 1,305); Nigeria (Ibadan) 3.8 / 0.9 (n = 769 M, 866 F); Ethiopia 3.6 / 0.6 (n = 844); Iran 3.5 / 1.0 (n = 1,069 M, 1,081 F); Thailand 3.7 / 0.5 (n = 3,056); Nepal 3.8 to 3.9 / 0; Caucasian children (Birch and Platts) 6.6 / 0.4 (n = 258 M, 255 F). Deutans were more frequent than protans in both sexes in the Durban sample (protan 0.7%, deutan 1.5% overall; 1.5% vs 2.7% in boys) [MODERATE] — [PMC6558866 Table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6558866/)
- A 2025 meta-analysis of 56 studies of children and adolescents (Ophthalmology) estimated global congenital CVD prevalence at 2.59%, with subgroup analyses by deutan/protan/tritan types; I could not retrieve the type- or sex-specific pooled figures [MODERATE; headline figure from search-tool summary only] — [PubMed 40769301](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40769301/)
- NEI describes the types: deuteranomaly is the most common red-green type and is usually mild; protanomaly makes some reds look greener and less bright; tritanopia makes blue/green, purple/red and yellow/pink hard to tell apart and colors look less bright; achromatopsia (monochromacy) is rare and may bring poor acuity and light sensitivity. The page gives no prevalence numbers [STRONG, descriptive] — [NEI: Types of color vision deficiency](https://www.nei.nih.gov/learn-about-eye-health/eye-conditions-and-diseases/color-blindness/types-color-vision-deficiency)
- Machado et al. (2009) note CVD affects approximately 200 million people worldwide [WEAK as an epidemiological claim; a motivation statement in a graphics paper] — [Machado, Oliveira, Fernandes 2009 via search summary](https://www.inf.ufrgs.br/~oliveira/gmmachado.html)

### Inferences
- Design for roughly 1 in 12 men and 1 in 200 women (about 4% of a mixed audience; Okabe and Ito's 400 per 10,000 estimate agrees) with deuteranomaly as the single most likely case, then protan types. Tritan and achromatopsia can be handled by guaranteeing a luminance (lightness) and non-color cue rather than by hue tuning, because they are about 100 times rarer.
- Because deutan and protan together account for nearly all CVD, a palette that survives deutan and protan simulation covers nearly all CVD users.

### Gaps
- Birch (2012) country-by-country numbers and Sharpe/Deeb et al. (1999, Cambridge UP, ch. 1, "Opsin genes, cone photopigments, color vision, and color blindness") per-type male/female figures (commonly quoted as about 1% protanopia, 1% protanomaly, 1% deuteranopia, 5% deuteranomaly in men): not retrieved; the authoritative per-type table is therefore unverified. Note the 1999 chapter is by Deeb et al. in a volume edited by Gegenfurtner and Sharpe.
- No retrieved figure for achromatopsia prevalence beyond "rare" and Wikipedia's unsourced "<0.0001%". Commonly cited about 1 in 30,000 is from background knowledge, unverified.
- No retrieved recent large-cohort data for the UK Biobank or US populations.

## 2. WCAG 2.2 requirements, weaknesses of WCAG 2 contrast, dark backgrounds and halation

### Takeaway
WCAG 2.2 requires 4.5:1 for normal text (3:1 for large text), 3:1 for non-text UI/graphics, color not as the only means of conveying information, and tolerance for user text-spacing overrides [STRONG, normative]. WCAG 2's luminance-ratio formula is hue-blind and polarity-symmetric, which critics say overstates contrast for dark colors; APCA is a draft alternative but not a standard [WEAK to MODERATE]. Dark text on a light background reads better than the reverse in several lab studies [MODERATE]; the halation/astigmatism mechanism is plausible but I could not verify it from primary sources [WEAK].

### Cited Findings
- **1.4.3 Contrast (Minimum), AA:** text and images of text at least 4.5:1; large text (at least 18 pt, or 14 pt bold) at least 3:1; exceptions for inactive UI, pure decoration, invisible text, text within a picture with significant other content, and logotypes. Ratio = (L1 + 0.05)/(L2 + 0.05), range 1:1 to 21:1, with sRGB luminance L = 0.2126 R + 0.7152 G + 0.0722 B (channels linearized). Thresholds are not rounded (4.499:1 fails). [STRONG] — [W3C Understanding 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)
- Same page: for people with CVD, hue and saturation have minimal or no effect on legibility and the deficiency does not impair light-dark contrast perception, so contrast is computed without regard to hue, with one noted exception: predominantly long-wavelength colors (reds) against dark colors can be difficult for protanopia [STRONG as W3C statement] — [W3C Understanding 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html). Okabe and Ito likewise advise avoiding red text on black, because red does not appear bright to color-blind viewers — [jfly.uni-koeln.de/color](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/)
- **1.4.6 Contrast (Enhanced), AAA:** 7:1 for text, 4.5:1 for large text; if a text color is specified, a background color must also be specified (and vice versa); the page does not mention dark mode, and says authors need not consider user-agent color changes except where caused by author code [STRONG] — [W3C Understanding 1.4.6](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-enhanced.html)
- **1.4.11 Non-text Contrast, AA:** UI components' identifying information and graphical objects needed to understand content must reach 3:1 against adjacent colors; lines in graphs need 3:1 against background but not against each other if they rarely overlap; a chart need not conform if labels and values convey the same information as text; focus indicators need 3:1 [STRONG] — [W3C Understanding 1.4.11](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/non-text-contrast.html)
- **1.4.1 Use of Color, A:** color must not be the only visual means of conveying information, indicating an action, prompting a response or distinguishing a visual element. Color coding is allowed when paired with another indicator; a lightness difference of at least 3:1 counts as an extra visual distinction; inline links need 3:1 against surrounding text (technique G183) unless another cue exists. Examples of non-color cues: icon with red text for required fields, numbers and legend, patterns [STRONG] — [W3C Understanding 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)
- **1.4.12 Text Spacing, AA:** content must not lose content or function when the user sets line height at least 1.5x font size, paragraph spacing at least 2x, letter spacing at least 0.12x, and word spacing at least 0.16x; this is a floor the user may exceed, and authors need not supply a control [STRONG] — [W3C Understanding 1.4.12](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/text-spacing.html). Implication in the same document: adaptable spacing makes it easier for users to apply other style preferences.
- **Known weaknesses of WCAG 2 contrast (advocacy-heavy sources):** APCA's author and supporters argue WCAG 2's ratio far overstates contrast for dark colors (a 4.5:1 pair near black may be hard to read) and is symmetric to polarity, so an inverted theme gets identical scores. APCA yields a signed Lc value; draft thresholds quoted by one source are about Lc 75 for body text, 60 for content text, 45 for large text, 30 for non-text UI; WCAG 3 is still a working draft and APCA is not a standard. Equivalences (Lc 45 about 3:1; Lc 75 about 7:1) differ by source and version. [WEAK; no independent W3C-confirmed numbers retrieved] — [APCA introduction (author's repo)](https://github.com/Myndex/apca-introduction); [SAPC-APCA "Why APCA"](https://www.github.com/Myndex/SAPC-APCA/blob/master/documentation/WhyAPCA.md); [AppCrib blog (secondary)](https://appcrib.pages.dev/blog/wcag-vs-apca-dark-mode-disagreement/); [Yatil, "WCAG 3 is not ready yet" (secondary)](https://www.yatil.net/blog/wcag-3-is-not-ready-yet)
- **Polarity, primary studies:** Buchner and Baumgartner (2007, Ergonomics 50(7):1036-63): proofreading was consistently better with positive polarity (dark text on light) than negative polarity, regardless of ambient illumination (dark room vs office) and chromaticity (black/white vs blue/yellow); a final experiment showed red-on-green color contrast could not compensate for missing luminance contrast [MODERATE; abstract-level, sample sizes unretrieved] — [Buchner and Baumgartner 2007, DOI 10.1080/00140130701306413, via search-tool summary of its abstract](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140130701306413)
- Piepenbrock, Mayr and Buchner (2013, Ergonomics 56(7)): visual acuity and proofreading both showed a positive-polarity advantage for younger and older adults; the proposed mechanism is brighter displays leading to smaller pupils and a sharper retinal image; the authors note the advantage could shrink for older adults with cataract, who were not tested [MODERATE; sample sizes unretrieved] — [Piepenbrock et al. 2013, DOI 10.1080/00140139.2013.790485 via search summary](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140139.2013.790485)
- **Halation / astigmatism / dark mode readability:** My searches returned mostly content-marketing pages and one optometrist-authored source saying research is mixed; the corneal-scatter explanation for bright-text-on-dark blur is plausible but I could not verify any primary study, and some astigmatic users reportedly prefer dark mode. [WEAK; no primary source retrieved] — summarized from low-quality search results (not cited as evidence). Treat the claim as an unverified hypothesis.
- **Computed contrast ratios (my calculation using the WCAG formula; all hexes from sections 3):**
  - Okabe-Ito colors vs white / vs black / vs #121212 / vs #1E1E1E: orange E69F00 2.25 / 9.32 / 8.32 / 7.40; sky blue 56B4E9 2.31 / 9.10 / 8.12 / 7.22; bluish green 009E73 3.42 / 6.14 / 5.48 / 4.87; yellow F0E442 1.32 / 15.88 / 14.17 / 12.61; blue 0072B2 5.19 / 4.05 / 3.61 / 3.21; vermillion D55E00 3.87 / 5.43 / 4.84 / 4.31; reddish purple CC79A7 3.06 / 6.86 / 6.12 / 5.45.
  - Tol "bright" vs white / vs black: 4477AA 4.70 / 4.46; EE6677 3.09 / 6.80; 228833 4.53 / 4.64; CCBB44 1.95 / 10.76; 66CCEE 1.84 / 11.44; AA3377 6.09 / 3.45; BBBBBB 1.92 / 10.94.
  - Tol "light" vs black (for dark-text-on-chip use): 77AADD 8.58; EE8866 8.35; EEDD88 15.33; FFAABB 11.72; 99DDFF 14.14; 44BB99 8.81; BBCC33 11.80; AAAA00 8.46; DDDDDD 15.46 (all also well below 3:1 on white, 1.36 to 2.52).
- **Dark text on bright chip vs colored text on dark ground:** Tol states his bright/vibrant/muted/etc. schemes are "too strong to use for backgrounds to mark (black) text"; for backgrounds use his "pale" scheme or "light" scheme; the "dark" scheme is for text and lines on white (contrast with white at least 4.5 for moderately low vision); the "light" scheme is not meant for lines on white [MODERATE; expert design note] — [Tol, colour schemes](https://sronpersonalpages.nl/~pault/). Okabe and Ito advise bright text over a dark background or vice versa (a brightness difference), avoid same-brightness hue-only pairs (red on green), and use bold sans-serif rather than thin serif fonts for color-coded text [MODERATE] — [jfly.uni-koeln.de/color](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/)
- **Forced colors / high contrast:** In `forced-colors: active` the browser overrides `color`, `background-color`, `text-decoration-color`, `border-color`, `outline-color`, SVG fill/stroke at paint time, forces `box-shadow` and `text-shadow` to none and non-url `background-image` to none, forces `color-scheme` to `light dark`, and may set `prefers-color-scheme` from the Canvas color lightness; `forced-color-adjust: none` opts an element out, and explicit system colors are respected; backplates are drawn behind text for legibility [STRONG, documentation] — [MDN forced-colors](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/forced-colors)

### Inferences
- A token that is colored text on a dark ground should be checked against 4.5:1 for the actual surface, not just against pure black (Okabe-Ito blue falls to 3.2 to 4.05 on dark surfaces, so it needs a lightened variant there; most other Okabe-Ito hues clear 4.5:1 on #121212 except vermillion at 4.84, which passes but only narrowly, and which Okabe/Ito and W3C flag as risky for protanopes).
- On a white or light surface almost all Okabe-Ito/Tol-bright hues fail 4.5:1 as text (only blue 5.19 and Tol 4477AA 4.70, 228833 4.53, AA3377 6.09 pass), so on light backgrounds either use darkened variants for text or use the saturated hue as a fill/underline/border (3:1 applies) with dark text on a pale tint.
- Dark text (black or near-black) on a saturated Okabe-Ito chip passes 4.5:1 for orange, sky blue, bluish green, yellow, vermillion, reddish purple, but not blue (4.05); white text on blue passes (5.19). Compute per-pair rather than assuming.
- Because the WCAG luminance ratio is hue-blind and polarity-symmetric, a 4.5:1 result is a floor, not proof of good readability on dark surfaces. Aim for margin (about 7:1 or more) for body-size colored text on dark; this is an inference from the weak APCA critique plus the polarity studies, not a tested rule.

### Gaps
- No primary peer-reviewed study retrieved on halation/irradiation or astigmatism and light-on-dark text; the claim stays WEAK. Candidates to check next (named from background knowledge, unverified): Dobres et al. (2016) on luminance polarity and font weight, and Legge/Rubin-style contrast-polarity reading-speed studies.
- APCA thresholds and equations were not verified against the current WCAG 3 draft text; no independent perceptual validation study of APCA vs WCAG 2 was retrieved.
- No source retrieved that tests colored (tinted) highlight backgrounds specifically for reading speed.
- Sample sizes for Buchner and Baumgartner (2007) and Piepenbrock et al. (2013) not retrieved.

## 3. Palettes proven for CVD, category limits, and simulation/validation tools

### Takeaway
The Okabe-Ito eight-color palette (as adapted by Wong 2011) is the most widely used CVD-safe categorical set; Paul Tol's schemes add tested options for 3, 6, 7 and 9 colors, with explicit contrast and background guidance. About 7 to 9 simultaneously distinct categorical colors is the practical ceiling for CVD-safe sets; beyond that, redundant cues are required.

### Cited Findings
- **Okabe-Ito / Wong 8-color palette** [MODERATE to STRONG; expert-designed and extensively adopted, designed by testing with color-blind observers]. Hex values: orange E69F00, sky blue 56B4E9, bluish green (blue-green) 009E73, yellow F0E442, blue 0072B2, vermillion D55E00, reddish purple (pale violet) CC79A7, plus black 000000. The seven chromatic hexes were read from a search-tool summary of a handout reproducing Wong (2011); black #000000 is the eighth color but was not in the retrieved text (see Gaps). Okabe and Ito's own page says vermilion is "#FF2000" (RGB 100%, 32%, 0%) and light red "#FF1414", which differs from the Wong D55E00 version; Krzywinski notes the original source's RGB table did not exactly match its swatches, probably an RGB/CMYK conversion mix-up. — [Wong 2011 summary](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/wong-pointsofviewcolorblindness); [Okabe and Ito](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/); [Krzywinski palettes](https://mk.bcgsc.ca/colorblind/palettes.mhtml)
- Wong's advice: replace red with magenta, or green with turquoise, to improve visibility for red-green deficiency; test designs with CVD simulation software [MODERATE] — [Wong 2011 summary](https://bibbase.org/network/publication/wong-pointsofviewcolorblindness)
- Okabe and Ito: use warm and cool colors alternately; when two colors are both warm or both cool, make brightness or saturation differ distinctly; avoid low-saturation and low-brightness combinations; use solid vs dotted lines, different symbols, hatching, thicker lines, direct labels; "Do not convey information in color only"; their companion paper aimed at "four easily distinguishable colors for all color vision types" [MODERATE] — [jfly.uni-koeln.de/color](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/)
- **Tol qualitative schemes (colour-blind safe unless noted):**
  - Bright, 7: 4477AA, EE6677, 228833, CCBB44, 66CCEE, AA3377, BBBBBB
  - High-contrast, 3: 004488, DDAA33, BB5566 (also works in greyscale)
  - Vibrant, 7: EE7733, 0077BB, 33BBEE, EE3377, CC3311, 009988, BBBBBB
  - Muted, 9: CC6677, 332288, DDCC77, 117733, 88CCEE, 882255, 44AA99, 999933, AA4499 (lacks a clear red or medium blue)
  - Medium-contrast, 6: 6699CC, 004488, EECC66, 994455, 997700, EE99AA (smaller differences)
  - Dark, 11 (for text/lines on white; only some cost for CVD users): 4477BB, CC4466, 228833, 2255AA, 993366, 006644, 117788, 882288, BB4488, 775500, 444444
  - Light, 9 (reasonably distinct in normal and CVD vision; for backgrounds): 77AADD, EE8866, EEDD88, FFAABB, 99DDFF, 44BB99, BBCC33, AAAA00, DDDDDD
  - Largest minimum contrast ratios between scheme colors: 2.8 for two colors, 2.1 for three, 1.5 for six (medium-contrast 1.4); other schemes "fail the contrast-ratio criterion completely". For more than nine distinct colors Tol suggests a sequential/banded scheme rather than a qualitative one. [MODERATE; expert source] — [Tol](https://sronpersonalpages.nl/~pault/)
- **Krzywinski larger sets:** 12-color palette maps four colors onto each of two color dimensions for deuteranopes and four onto greyscale (useful for transit maps); other palettes were built to keep perceptual luminance uniform in color-blind space, except the 8-color one; 15- and 24-color sets exist [WEAK to MODERATE; personal site] — [Krzywinski](https://mk.bcgsc.ca/colorblind/palettes.mhtml)
- **Category limits:** Lecture slides citing Ware's Information Visualization recommend about 12 or fewer hues for nominal coding (another deck attributed to Ware says more than 12 will likely not work and about 5 is recommended); Healey's 1996 UBC report notes earlier military guidance of five to seven colors with no stated rationale and proposes a method to maximize the number of colors while keeping rapid, accurate search [WEAK for the exact number because secondhand; the order of magnitude (5 to 12) is consistent across sources] — [Ware lecture notes](https://www.aviz.fr/wiki/uploads/TeachingVA2017/Lecture10Perception.pdf); [Healey, TR-96-10](https://www.cs.ubc.ca/tr/1996/tr-96-10)
- **CVD simulation:** Machado, Oliveira and Fernandes (2009, IEEE TVCG 15(6):1291-98) present a physiologically based model unifying normal vision, anomalous trichromacy and dichromacy, validated with groups of CVD and normal-vision observers; it covers protanopia, deuteranopia, protanomaly, deuteranomaly. The R package colorspace implements it (deutan, protan, tritan, with severity 0 to 1, 1 = dichromacy; linear-RGB default since v2.1-0) and cites Viénot et al. (1995, 1999), Brettel et al. (1997) and Lumley (2006, dichromat) as earlier methods; another R package, colorBlindness, uses Viénot's methods [STRONG that these methods exist and are implemented] — [Machado et al.](https://www.inf.ufrgs.br/~oliveira/gmmachado.html); [colorspace CVD vignette](https://colorspace.r-forge.r-project.org/articles/color_vision_deficiency.html); [colorspace JSS paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1903.06490); [colorBlindness cvdSimulator](https://search.r-project.org/CRAN/refmans/colorBlindness/html/cvdSimulator.html)

### Inferences
- A practical categorical budget for a text-styling tool is 6 to 8 hues on a given surface, drawn from Okabe-Ito (8 incl. black) or Tol bright/vibrant (7) / muted (9); beyond that, add non-color cues rather than more hues. This is an inference from the above palette sizes (Tol's ceiling of 9 for qualitative schemes, Okabe-Ito 8) and the 5 to 12 range in secondary sources, not a measured limit.
- Subsets: Tol high-contrast (3) is the safest minimal triple; Okabe and Ito's four-color target suggests a 4-hue core (e.g., blue 0072B2, orange E69F00, bluish green 009E73, reddish purple CC79A7 / vermillion) with remaining hues as lower-priority; remaining pairs most at risk under deutan/protan simulation are vermillion vs bluish green and bluish green vs yellow-orange when lightness is similar, so keep lightness steps between neighbors.
- Okabe-Ito hexes are tuned as fills/lines on white/black. For text-on-surface use, derive per-surface variants (darkened for light, lightened for dark) and re-verify contrast and deutan/protan separation with a simulator.

### Gaps
- Black #000000 as the eighth Okabe-Ito color and the original-paper color names are from background knowledge; the retrieved summary listed only seven. Verify at the Wong 2011 paper (nmeth.1618) or Okabe and Ito's Fig. 16 image.
- Color Oracle, Coblis and Viz Palette: no sources retrieved. (Background knowledge only: Color Oracle is a desktop simulator for deutan/protan/tritan; Coblis is a web image simulator; Viz Palette is a web tool to preview palettes with CVD simulations. Verify before citing.) ColorBrewer and Viridis claims (ColorBrewer's CVD-safe flag and qualitative palette sizes, Viridis designed for CVD) were not searched; Harrower and Brewer 2003 and Smith/van der Walt not retrieved.
- No empirical study retrieved giving a measured maximum number of CVD-distinguishable text colors (as opposed to patches/lines/points). Small text glyph colors are harder to discriminate than large patches (small-field tritanopia effects are a known vision-science point, but I did not retrieve a source).
- Ware's book itself was not accessed; 12-hue figure is secondhand.

## 4. Redundant coding: non-color cues, evidence of benefit, limits on stacking

### Takeaway
Redundant coding (color plus a second dimension) is endorsed by WCAG 1.4.1 and by expert color-design guidance, and is reported to raise identification accuracy and speed, in part depending on familiarity [MODERATE, mixed results]. I found no ranking of text-specific cues (weight vs underline vs italic vs tint) by reliability or reading interference, and no tested limit on stacking cues [NONE FOUND].

### Cited Findings
- A NASA technical document summary (via search tool) says redundant coding in a multidimensional stimulus has been demonstrated to increase identification accuracy and decrease identification latency compared with single-dimension stimuli, and that the benefit is in part a function of familiarity [MODERATE; secondary summary, PDF was a scanned image I could not read] — [NASA NTRS 19920024935](https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19920024935/downloads/19920024935.pdf)
- Christ (1975, Human Factors 17(6):542-70) is the standard review of color-coding effects on visual search and identification; I confirmed it exists and its aim, but could not retrieve its specific findings on redundant coding [STRONG that it exists; findings unretrieved] — [Christ 1975 abstract record](https://scilit.com/publications/17ff1ae4b164881a6f549402f006c1b5)
- Mixed result: a 1982 signal-detection study of shape-color redundant coding found neither objective nor subjective measures were affected by coding conditions; a 1997 study (McFadden and Bauer) found search was best under a linearly separable color coding, reduced under non-linearly separable, and worst with no color coding [WEAK to MODERATE; abstract-level] — [1982 study listing](https://www.sciencegate.app/document/10.1177/154193128202600622); [1997 McFadden and Bauer listing](https://searchworks.jioinstitute.edu.in/articles/edsair__edsair.doi...........2a7c898e7b6406c19953772e3d67faa7)
- Non-color cues endorsed by authoritative sources: WCAG lists icons, text labels, numbers with a legend, patterns, and a 3:1 lightness difference as acceptable additional means ([W3C 1.4.1](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/use-of-color.html)); Okabe and Ito list line style, symbols, hatching, thickness/size, direct labels, and bold sans-serif for color-coded text ([jfly](https://jfly.uni-koeln.de/color/)). Neither ranks cues by distinguishability.
- Typographic emphasis and comprehension: in a second-language study (LaBrozzi 2014, n = 109) readers saw one kind of textual enhancement (underline, bold, italics, font size, capitals, or a font change); one type helped form recognition more than the others and comprehension was not hindered by any type [WEAK to MODERATE; search summary] — [LaBrozzi 2014 listing](https://vc.bridgew.edu/languages_fac/21). A University of Reading eye-movement study of bolding within words (Bionic-style) found no differences in word skipping, reading times, or sentence reading speed/comprehension [MODERATE; comprehension near ceiling] — [Reading CentAUR 130289](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/130289/). A survey-attention study found bold and underline most effective at capturing attention [WEAK; market-research source] — [YouGov](https://business.yougov.com/content/51784-impact-of-text-formatting-on-survey-attention). Overall: emphasis mainly changes attention and memory for the emphasized items and has little effect on overall comprehension.
- Highlighting/underlining for learning: Dunlosky et al. (2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest) rated highlighting and underlining as low utility (found no benefit beyond plain reading, and possibly worse for inference), versus high utility for distributed practice and practice testing [STRONG for the review; applies to student-applied highlighting of study text, not to automatic semantic styling] — [Dunlosky 2013 summaries](https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2013/01/130110111734.htm)

### Inferences
- Pair every semantic color with at least one cue that survives grayscale/CVD: font weight, underline style, or a tint/border/icon. Prefer cues that do not change glyph widths or line breaks (underline style, border/outline, background tint, small marker glyph) over cues that do (weight, italics, font family, small caps), since layout shifts could interfere with reading; this is a design inference, with no study retrieved comparing these.
- Limit how many cues stack on one token (suggest at most color plus one other cue, perhaps two) and how many distinct cue classes appear per paragraph. The Dunlosky finding that heavy marking draws attention to isolated facts and the Bionic-reading null result are the only retrieved hints; there is no direct evidence on a stacking limit.
- Because highlighting-type emphasis has low learning utility when applied indiscriminately, a styling tool should be sparse and meaning-bearing, not decorative.

### Gaps
- Wickens (human factors, redundancy gain and the "redundant coding" literature), Ware's book chapter on color/texture/shape channels, and Christ (1975)'s actual figures were not retrieved. No quantified redundancy gain (milliseconds or percent) retrieved.
- No study retrieved ranking underline style, weight, italics, small caps, tint, border, or icon for discriminability within running text or their reading cost. Stroop-like interference from color-word semantics (color-named words styled in conflicting colors) not researched.
- No source on limits to stacking cues [NONE FOUND].

## 5. Semantic color consistency, learning, harm of changing mappings, color-concept associations, personalization

### Takeaway
Semantically resonant (intuitive) color assignments speed chart reading relative to arbitrary palettes [MODERATE, Lin et al. 2013]; consistent stimulus-response mappings can become automatic with practice while varied mappings do not [MODERATE, classic cognitive psychology]. I found no direct measurement of how long a new color-meaning code takes to learn in interfaces, no study of the harm of remapping, and no study comparing user-chosen with designer-chosen codes [NONE FOUND].

### Cited Findings
- Lin, Fortuna, Kulkarni, Stone and Heer (2013, EuroVis / Computer Graphics Forum, DOI 10.1111/cgf.12127): an algorithm picks colors for categorical values from color-concept affinity derived from representative images, trading off color probability against discriminability; in a controlled study, expert-chosen semantic colors sped up chart reading compared with a standard palette and the algorithm's colors gave similar gains; a second study showed it generalizes across categories. Participant counts and speed-up figures not retrieved. [MODERATE] — [UW IDL](https://idl.uw.edu/papers/semantically-resonant-colors)
- Palmer and Schloss (2010, PNAS 107:8877-82): the ecological valence theory says color preference comes from average affective responses to objects of that color (blues liked for sky and water, browns disliked for associations like feces and rotten food); it predicted about 80% of the variance in average color preference (via a conference follow-up); the PNAS study used 48 participants rating 32 colors. This is about preference, not about warning/ok semantics. [MODERATE for preference, and not direct evidence for red-warning/green-go mappings] — [PubMed 20421475](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20421475/); [Palmer and Schloss PMC2889342](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2889342)
- Schloss, Gramazio, Silverman, Parker and Wang (2019, IEEE TVCG 25(1)): in colormap visualizations people infer color-quantity mappings with a dark-is-more bias when opacity does not seem to vary and an opaque-is-more bias when it does; these biases agree on light backgrounds and conflict on dark backgrounds. So mappings inferred by viewers depend on the background. [MODERATE; abstract-level] — [Schloss lab summary](https://schlosslab.discovery.wisc.edu/new-publication-mapping-color-to-meaning-in-colormap-data-visualizations)
- Schneider and Shiffrin (1977, Psychological Review 84): after training under consistent mapping (items always targets or always distractors), increases in memory load and display size had little effect on search performance, whereas under varied mapping both hurt; the stimulus-to-response mapping determines whether a task can become automatic. Critiques: Chow (1986) suggested a confound in display-size functions; a 2008 study found consistent-mapping training neither necessary nor sufficient for optimal search. The training amount in the original study was many hundreds of trials (figure not retrieved). [MODERATE as general cognitive principle; not tested on color-coded text specifically] — [Schneider and Shiffrin 1977 summary](https://www.jimdavies.org/summaries/schneider1977.html); [search listing of critiques](https://link.springer.com/article/10.3758/PP.70.8.1401)
- Self-generated vs system-assigned mappings: the nearest result was a learner self-directed "4-colour memory" vocabulary strategy (thesis, no comparison retrieved) and a study finding performance was significantly better without color coding even though participants felt it would help (older short-term memory study), which suggests perceived benefit may not match measured performance [WEAK; search summary] — [search results incl. Oxford thesis and PMC9674548](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9674548/)
- Color-coded vs grayscale: a 2021 EEG and eye-tracking study found a color-coded design more beneficial than grayscale for programming lectures (compares color vs none, not who chose the colors) [WEAK; abstract-level] — [Frontiers in Psychology 2021 (773328)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.773328/pdf)

### Inferences
- Stable mappings are better than shifting ones: because consistent mapping enables automaticity (Schneider and Shiffrin) and semantically resonant colors speed reading (Lin et al.), a styling tool should keep the color-to-meaning table fixed across documents and sessions, and treat any remap as a deliberate, user-initiated, versioned change. The size of the harm from remapping is not measured in anything I retrieved.
- Where defaults are chosen, prefer conventional associations (e.g., warm/red for danger or warning, green for OK, blue for information) because they probably reduce learning cost; however, I did not retrieve a source quantifying these specific associations or cultural differences, so this is inference. Culture-dependent associations are an expected risk for non-Western users.
- Self-chosen mappings may be remembered better (generation-effect reasoning) but I found no direct evidence for it. Offering user overrides is justified mainly by accessibility needs (CVD-specific palettes) and preference, not by proven memory benefit; ship stable defaults, let the user remap once, and then freeze the mapping.
- Inferred color-quantity mappings flip with background (Schloss 2019), so if the tool uses lightness or opacity to show magnitude, test on both light and dark surfaces or use discrete categorical cues.

### Gaps
- No retrieved source quantifying red = warning, green = go, blue = information associations (Palmer/Schloss color-concept association work beyond preference; Elliot and Maier on red; cross-cultural studies), or cultural differences (e.g., red as good luck in East Asian cultures, red and green in finance markets reversed).
- No retrieved source on time to learn an arbitrary color code (sessions/trials) in interface, aviation, medical or code-editor contexts; no aviation or medical standard (e.g., MIL-STD-1472, FAA color-coding guidance, alarm colors in IEC 60601-1-8) retrieved. The FAA AM92-06 report appeared in search results but was not read.
- No retrieved experiment on changing a learned color mapping (negative transfer, interference), nor on user-chosen vs designer-chosen mappings in interfaces or code editors.
- Syntax-highlighting studies (e.g., Hannebauer et al. 2018; Beelders and du Plessis 2016; Sarkar 2015) were not searched.

## 6. User preference, dark/light mode, and defaults (prefers-color-scheme, forced-colors, high contrast)

### Takeaway
Controlled studies show a performance advantage for dark text on a light background for proofreading and acuity [MODERATE], while I found no reliable evidence on preference vs performance trade-offs for dark mode; the web platform exposes `prefers-color-scheme` and `forced-colors`, and in forced-colors mode author colors are overridden, so non-color cues must carry the meaning.

### Cited Findings
- Positive polarity advantage in proofreading and acuity, independent of ambient light and of black/white vs blue/yellow color pairs (Buchner and Baumgartner 2007), and shown for both younger and older adults (Piepenbrock et al. 2013) [MODERATE; see section 2 for citations] — [Buchner and Baumgartner](https://doi.org/10.1080/00140130701306413); [Piepenbrock et al.](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140139.2013.790485)
- Forced colors mode: enabled via user OS settings like Windows High Contrast; browser may set `prefers-color-scheme` from Canvas lightness; author color on text, backgrounds, borders, decoration colors, SVG fills are replaced by system colors (CanvasText, ButtonText, etc.); text/box shadows and gradient backgrounds are removed; `forced-color-adjust: none` opts out and disables backplates; MDN guidance: use the media query for small fixes, not a separate design [STRONG, documentation] — [MDN forced-colors](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/CSS/@media/forced-colors)
- WCAG 1.4.3 non-normative note allows user-adjustable colors, including lower-contrast settings for people with cognitive disabilities, provided a mechanism returns to the required values [STRONG] — [W3C 1.4.3](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Understanding/contrast-minimum.html)
- Low-quality results (content-marketing pages) claim dark mode helps in dim rooms and comfort while light mode edges reading accuracy; these are unverified and not cited as evidence.

### Inferences
- Defaults: follow `prefers-color-scheme` (and `color-scheme`) for the surface; honor `prefers-contrast` and `forced-colors`; under forced colors drop hue-based semantics and rely on the redundant cue (weight, underline style, borders, icons or text markers), since fills, shadows and gradients vanish and text colors map to a small system palette. Avoid carrying meaning in `background-color` or `box-shadow` alone.
- Since performance studies favor dark-on-light text, and preference evidence was not found, expose the dark/light choice to the user and keep the style semantics identical across both modes (same cues, same token names, per-surface color variants), rather than auto-inverting colors.
- Keep text-spacing overrides working (1.4.12 values) when the tool injects spans, borders or padding.

### Gaps
- No retrieved survey or experiment on actual user preference for dark vs light mode, or on preference-performance divergence (e.g., NN/g's review, Dobres et al. 2016); content-marketing claims of a "2024 Nielsen Norman Group meta-analysis" could not be verified.
- `prefers-color-scheme`, `prefers-contrast`, `prefers-reduced-transparency` and `color-scheme` semantics were not independently fetched beyond the MDN forced-colors page (which did not mention prefers-contrast).

## Implications for a text-styling tool

Format: **[Evidence]** is directly supported by the cited findings above; **[Inference]** is my reasoning from that evidence and is not tested. Labels give strength.

- **[Evidence, STRONG]** Design for about 8% of men and 0.4 to 0.5% of women (Northern European ancestry), lower in other groups; deutan and protan types dominate; tritan and achromatopsia are about 1 in 10,000 or rarer (section 1).
- **[Evidence, STRONG]** Never use color alone: WCAG 1.4.1 requires another visual means; a 3:1 lightness difference counts; text needs 4.5:1 (3:1 large), non-text indicators need 3:1; the tool must survive user text-spacing overrides (1.4.12: 1.5 line height, 0.12 letter spacing, 0.16 word spacing, 2x paragraph spacing) (section 2).
- **[Evidence, MODERATE]** Use an established CVD-safe palette: Okabe-Ito/Wong (orange E69F00, sky blue 56B4E9, bluish green 009E73, yellow F0E442, blue 0072B2, vermillion D55E00, reddish purple CC79A7, black 000000 [black unretrieved]) or Tol bright (4477AA, EE6677, 228833, CCBB44, 66CCEE, AA3377, BBBBBB) / muted (9 colors) (section 3).
- **[Evidence, MODERATE]** Tol advises that saturated qualitative schemes are too strong as backgrounds behind black text and that his "light" (9) or "pale" scheme be used for tint backgrounds; Okabe and Ito advise bright-on-dark or dark-on-bright with a brightness difference, avoiding hue-only pairs and red text on black, and using bold sans-serif for color-coded text (section 2).
- **[Evidence, computed by me from the WCAG formula]** Most Okabe-Ito hues pass 4.5:1 as text on near-black (#121212) but only blue passes on white; black text on the saturated chips passes for every hue except blue (4.05), and Tol "light" hues give 8.3 to 15.5:1 with black text (section 2). Verify any final hex pair in code.
- **[Evidence, MODERATE]** Dark text on a light background gave better proofreading and acuity than light on dark in lab studies, including for older adults, regardless of ambient light and color pairing; colour contrast could not substitute for luminance contrast (section 2). **[Evidence, WEAK]** The halation/astigmatism mechanism for dark backgrounds is unverified.
- **[Evidence, MODERATE, mixed]** Redundant coding (color plus another dimension) is reported to raise identification accuracy and cut latency, depending on familiarity, but at least one study found no effect; there is no quantified gain and no ranking of text-specific cues (section 4).
- **[Evidence, MODERATE]** Semantically resonant color assignments sped chart reading versus a standard palette (Lin et al. 2013); consistent mapping enables automatic processing while varied mapping does not (Schneider and Shiffrin 1977, with critiques) (section 5).
- **[Evidence, STRONG for platform behavior]** In forced-colors mode the browser replaces author text, background, border, decoration and SVG colors with system colors and removes shadows and gradient backgrounds, so color-only semantics are lost (section 6).
- **[Evidence, STRONG for the review, inapplicable to automation]** Student-applied highlighting has low learning utility (Dunlosky et al. 2013); emphasis mostly changes attention to the emphasized item with little effect on overall comprehension (sections 4).
- **[NONE FOUND]** No evidence for: how many cues may be stacked; how long a new color code takes to learn; harm of remapping; whether self-chosen mappings are remembered better; cultural variation of warning/ok/info colors (not retrieved); astigmatism-specific dark-mode findings from primary studies.

- **[Inference]** Pick a 6 to 8 hue semantic budget per surface (Okabe-Ito or Tol), assign meanings to the most separable hues first, and give each meaning a second cue that survives grayscale (underline style, border, tint, weight, icon/marker). Prefer cues that do not change line breaks (underline style, border, tint, marker) over weight or italics or font changes.
- **[Inference]** Generate per-surface variants: darkened hues for light backgrounds (to reach 4.5:1) and lightened hues for dark backgrounds (blue needs lightening); or use the saturated hue only as a fill/underline (3:1) with dark text on a pale tint (Tol light) on light backgrounds. Check every pair numerically (4.5:1 minimum; aim 7:1 or more on dark surfaces given APCA-style critique), and re-run deutan and protan simulation (Machado 2009 via colorspace) including a lightness-separation check.
- **[Inference]** Keep the color-to-meaning table stable across sessions and documents, based on consistent-mapping and semantic-resonance evidence; let users remap but freeze the result, log changes, and show a legend. Do not claim self-chosen mappings improve memory; that is untested here. Provide CVD-friendly presets (Okabe-Ito, Tol) rather than free hue picking as the default personalization path.
- **[Inference]** Default to `prefers-color-scheme`, honor `forced-colors` and `prefers-contrast`, keep semantics identical across light and dark (same cues, per-surface hues), and provide a visible user toggle; do not rely on background-color or shadows alone under forced colors.
- **[Inference]** Style sparsely: emphasis that is everywhere is not emphasis, and indiscriminate highlighting has low learning value. Limit simultaneous cue classes per paragraph and keep stacked cues to color plus one (maybe two) pending evidence.
- **[Inference]** Treat red text (vermillion, CC3311) on dark surfaces with caution for protanopes; pair with a non-color cue and, where possible, a lighter or more orange red.
