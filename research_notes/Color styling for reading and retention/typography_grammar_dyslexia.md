# Typography and Text-Presentation Interventions for Reading: Evidence Review

Evidence labels used throughout: **STRONG** = multiple independent well-powered studies or systematic reviews agree; **MODERATE** = a few studies / one good study plus partial replication; **WEAK** = single small study, conflicting results, or abstract-level only; **NO EVIDENCE** = nothing credible found, or credible evidence of no effect (stated explicitly which). "Verified" = I read the source page/abstract this session; "search-snippet only" = I saw it only in a search summary and did not open the primary paper. Practitioner/vendor sources are flagged as such.

Caveat on method: this was a ~35-call web pass, not a systematic review. Many figures come from abstracts or secondary summaries. Items I could not locate are listed under Gaps rather than filled from memory.

## 1. Typography basics (line length, spacing, size, serif/sans, dyslexia fonts, letter spacing, bold/italic, screen vs paper, polarity, phone vs desktop)

### Takeaway
Dyslexia-specific fonts (OpenDyslexic, Dyslexie) have no independent support; the one robust-ish typographic finding for dyslexic readers is wider letter/word spacing (reliable for accuracy, inconsistent for speed). Line length, serif vs sans, and font choice matter little for typical adults; screen-vs-paper has a small but real comprehension cost for expository text; polarity effects favor dark-on-light mainly for small text/acuity-type tasks and are inconsistent elsewhere.

### Cited Findings

**Dyslexia fonts**
- Wery & Diliberto (2017, Annals of Dyslexia): single-subject alternating-treatments design comparing OpenDyslexic with Arial and Times New Roman on letter naming, word reading and nonsense-word reading; no improvement in reading rate or accuracy for individuals or the group; no participant preferred OpenDyslexic. Small sample, elementary students. Label: WEAK-to-MODERATE evidence of *no benefit* (small N, but consistent with others). — [PMC5629233](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5629233/) (Edutopia says the font *reduced* speed/accuracy; that overstates the paper's "no improvement" finding — [Edutopia](https://www.edutopia.org/article/do-dyslexia-fonts-actually-work/) as reported in search results)
- Marinus et al. (2016, Dyslexia 22:233-244): children with dyslexia read faster in Dyslexie than in Arial (31/39 children read more words per minute), but when Arial's within-word and between-word spacing was matched to Dyslexie's, the difference was no longer significant; i.e., the benefit came from spacing, not letter shapes. Label: MODERATE (that shapes do not matter). — [PubMed 27194598](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27194598/); summary via [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/spacing-of-letters-not-shape-of-letters-slightly-increases-reading-speed-of-those-with-dyslexia-59316)
- Kuster, van Weerdenburg, van Gompel & Bosman (2017/2018, Annals of Dyslexia) concluded the Dyslexie font neither benefits nor impedes reading in children with or without dyslexia (independent replication of the null on letter shape; I only saw this via a search summary, details/N not verified). Label: MODERATE in combination with Marinus. — [UMich Dyslexia Help](https://dyslexiahelp.umich.edu/latest/does-dyslexie-font-help-dyslexic-readers/); [BrainFacts](https://www.brainfacts.org/Diseases-and-Disorders/Childhood-Disorders/2017/Can-Special-Fonts-Help-People-With-Dyslexia-082117)
- Vendor claim (flagged: marketing): the Dyslexie site states several studies show faster, more accurate reading with Dyslexie. Not supported by the independent peer-reviewed work above. — [dyslexiefont.com](https://dyslexiefont.com/en/research/)
- Rello & Baeza-Yates (2013, ASSETS): eye tracking with 48 people with dyslexia, 12 fonts, 12 texts. Sans-serif, monospaced and roman styles beat serif, proportional and italic; italics consistently worst; Helvetica, Courier, Arial, Verdana and Computer Modern Unicode named best; OpenDyslexic did not beat standard fonts. Label: MODERATE (one group, Spanish-speaking sample per their 2016 follow-up; effect sizes not captured here). — [search summary](https://blog.dyslexia.com/?p=872); [BYU summary](https://editingresearch.byu.edu/2020/05/28/which-fonts-are-best-for-dyslexia/)
- Rello & Baeza-Yates 2016 follow-up (TACCESS): 97 Spanish speakers (48 dyslexic, 49 not); sans-serif outperformed serif for dyslexic participants; monospaced led to shorter fixations. Search-snippet only. — [search summary](https://blog.dyslexia.com/?p=872)
- Lexend: design rationale from Shaver-Troup's work (wider spacing, expanded scaling, sans-serif). Only a doctoral study and a small (reported n=20 third graders) classroom test, mostly via design/marketing sites; claims like "19.8% fluency gain" are from secondary summaries. No peer-reviewed independent replication found. Label: NO EVIDENCE (beyond theory) for font-shape benefit; plausibly just a wide-spaced sans. — [Design Observer](https://designobserver.com/the-role-of-letterforms-in-reading-instruction/); [Bold/Text Control](https://bold.textcontrol.com/article/2019/8/14/a/lexend-a-font-is-changing-the-way-the-world-reads/) (practitioner/marketing)

**Letter spacing**
- Zorzi et al. (2012, PNAS 109:11455): dyslexic children 8-14 (34 Italian, 40 French); extra-large letter spacing yielded roughly 20% faster reading and about halved errors in text reading; poorest readers gained most; attributed to reduced visual crowding. — [PubMed 22665803](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22665803/); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2012/06/120607105712.htm)
- Replication is partial: Hakvoort et al. (2017, Dutch) reported fewer errors but no speed gain (second-hand via Galliussi et al. 2020); Perea et al. (2012, Spanish) reported ~10% faster and ~50% fewer errors (second-hand); a Tijms et al. conference abstract found no speed effect and that typical and dyslexic readers benefited equally; Galliussi et al. (2020) found speed was impaired by increased letter spacing unless word spacing was also increased. Label: MODERATE for accuracy gains in dyslexic children; WEAK/inconsistent for speed; WEAK for dyslexia-specificity. — [PMC7188700 (Galliussi et al., via search)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700); [Perea interletter pdf](https://www.uv.es/mperea/interletter_SJP.pdf)
- Stagg & Kiss (2021, Research in Developmental Disabilities): 59 children aged 11-15 (32 dyslexic, 27 not). Extra-large spacing improved speed by ~13% (dyslexic) and ~5% (non-dyslexic) and cut word omissions; coloured overlays had no significant effect on speed or errors. Short passages, small N. Label: MODERATE (adds a third independent-ish replication of spacing, plus a null for overlays). — [Westminster Research](https://westminsterresearch.westminster.ac.uk/item/x14xz/room-to-read-the-effect-of-extra-large-letter-spacing-and-coloured-overlays-on-reading-speed-and-accuracy-in-adolescents-with-dyslexia); [ScienceDaily](https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2021/09/210929212202.htm)
- Zorzi's spacing was 2.5 pt on a 14 pt body (about 18% of body size), per the Hakvoort/Galliussi description. — [PMC7188700](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7188700)

**Font size and line spacing (dyslexic readers; Rello et al.)**
- Rello, Kanvinde & Baeza-Yates (2012, W4A): 22 dyslexic participants; largest tested size (26 pt) preferred by ~64% with shorter fixations than 14 pt; suggested +7% character spacing and 1.4 line spacing (largely preference-driven). Rello et al. (2013, Wikipedia study): 28 dyslexic readers, six font sizes, four line spacings: font size significantly affected readability and comprehension (18 pt best); line spacing had no effect. The two studies disagree on line spacing. Label: WEAK-MODERATE for "bigger is better up to about 18 pt for dyslexic readers"; WEAK/no evidence for line spacing. — [search summary of Rello papers](https://a11y-paradise.onrender.com/reviews/69c083aebb27ccd8963e779f) (aggregator; primary papers not opened)

**Line length**
- Dyson & Haselgrove: comprehension peaked at ~55 characters per line on screen; 55-100 cpl had no effect on reading speed in a complex comprehension task. Shaikh & Chaparro (2005, n=20): 95 cpl read fastest among 35/55/75/95. Overall pattern: longer lines are read faster; comprehension effects small/inconsistent; preference tends toward shorter or longest lines. Label: MODERATE that the effect is small; the commonly quoted "45-75 cpl rule" is convention more than evidence. — [search results summary](https://www.blog.theteamw.com/2009/10/26/100-things-you-should-know-about-people-2-line-length/) (practitioner blog); [ERAU online news line-length study](https://portfolio.erau.edu/en/publications/the-effects-of-line-length-on-reading-performance-of-online-news-/)

**Screen vs paper**
- Delgado, Vargas, Ackerman & Salmeron (2018, Educational Research Review 25:23-38): meta-analysis of 54 studies (2000-2017) found paper superior for comprehension; effect larger for expository than narrative texts and under time pressure; computers vs handhelds did not differ significantly but few handheld studies; paper advantage appeared to grow over time. Label: STRONG that a small screen inferiority exists for expository text under time pressure (effect size not captured; check original). — [Haifa CRIS record](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/dont-throw-away-your-printed-books-a-meta-analysis-on-the-effects/); [Oxford Owl summary](https://www.oxfordowl.co.uk/welcome-back/for-school-back/pathways-page/pathwayslist/teaching-comprehension/background-questions-tc/wales--16/is-there-a-difference-between-reading-on-screen-and-reading-on-paper-in-terms-of-comprehension--2)

**Polarity / dark mode**
- Piepenbrock, Mayr, Mund & Buchner (2013, Ergonomics): acuity and proofreading tasks showed a positive-polarity (dark text on light) advantage in both younger and older adults; recommend positive polarity at all ages; older group did not include cataract patients. A related group paper links this to smaller pupil size and better proofreading with positive polarity. Label: MODERATE (replicated across multiple Buchner-group papers, but same lab). — [Ergonomics abstract](https://informahealthcare.com/doi/abs/10.1080/00140139.2013.790485); [Fundacion MAPFRE record](https://documentacion.fundacionmapfre.org/documentacion/publico/pt/bib/143702.do?format=xml)
- Buchner & Baumgartner (2007) and Buchner, Mayr & Brandt (2009) attribute the positive-polarity advantage to display luminance; a cited 2020 review found the advantage grew as font size shrank (could not confirm source). — [search summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11175232/)
- Counter-evidence: a 2024 study of 30 young adults (JHSMR) found faster reading with negative polarity (136 vs 128 wpm), no error difference. Label: WEAK (single small study, low-tier venue). — [JHSMR](https://jhsmr.org/index.php/jhsmr/article/view/1095)
- NN/g (practitioner) says its cited evidence shows light mode better, with the difference depending on lighting (no significant polarity effect in daytime; light mode better at night), and that it has done no dark-mode research itself. — [NN/g dark mode](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/dark-mode/)
- Astigmatism: I found no controlled study. The "dark mode is worse with astigmatism (halation)" claim appears only in popular-press and Alibaba "product insight" posts and optometry-adjacent commentary. Label: NO EVIDENCE (controlled) / mechanistically plausible only. — [search results; popular press](https://tidbits.com/2019/05/31/the-dark-side-of-dark-mode/)

**Bold/italic**
- Italics were consistently worst in Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013 (dyslexic readers; see above). For bold emphasis effects see section 5 and 7.

### Inferences
- Because the benefit of "dyslexia fonts" collapses once spacing is matched (Marinus), a tool should expose spacing (letter + word + line) rather than a "dyslexia font" toggle.
- Default to dark-on-light for small text, offer dark mode as a preference/ambient-light adaptation; not as a performance feature.
- The 55-75 cpl "rule" is a safe default, not a requirement.

### Gaps
- No controlled study found on astigmatism x polarity; effect sizes for Delgado 2018 not captured; Rello 2013/2016/2017 primary papers not opened.
- No good evidence found on phone vs desktop reading speed/comprehension specifically (Delgado reports handheld vs computer not different but few studies).
- No sourced evidence in this pass on serif vs sans for typical adult readers on screen (only the dyslexic-reader result above).
- Line spacing for typical readers: nothing sourced here.

## 2. Color-coded grammar (Montessori symbols, Colourful Semantics, color-coded syllables/morphology, L2 gender coding, textual enhancement)

### Takeaway
There is no strong, replicated evidence that coloring parts of speech or syllables improves reading or writing; what exists is small, mostly dissertation- or practitioner-level, and for L2 gender learning the results are positive but modest and uncontrolled for novelty. Montessori grammar symbols have no controlled evidence I could find. Colourful Semantics is rated "moderate" by a UK charity, but the evidence is small clinical studies, mostly DLD.

### Cited Findings

**Colourful Semantics**
- Bolderson, Dosanjh, Milligan, Pring & Chiat (2011), "Colourful semantics: A clinical investigation," Child Language Teaching and Therapy 27:344-353. I could not retrieve results. — [citation record](https://bvbvf1.bib-bvb.de/vufind/PrimoRecord/cdi_proquest_miscellaneous_908019176)
- Speech and Language UK rates the approach "Moderate" evidence; PenCRU (2018) found no studies on children with ASD; most research is on developmental language disorder. Small multiple-baseline clinic study (Wilson & Hervey) outcome measures RAPT and MLU. Label: WEAK (small N, case-series level; the color component is not isolated from the intervention). — [Speech and Language UK](https://speechandlanguage.org.uk/evidence-rating/moderate); [Exeter PenCRU](https://exeter.ac.uk/media/universityofexeter/medicalschool/subsites/pencru/pdfs/WTE_Colourful_Semantics.pdf); [RCSLT poster](https://www.rcslt.org/wp-content/uploads/media/Project/RCSLT/lorna-wilson.pdf)

**Montessori grammar symbols**
- No controlled study of the symbols or of their color coding found; sources are descriptive or promotional; a Montessori Public article states no Tier 1 evidence supports the Montessori reading curriculum. Label: NO EVIDENCE. — [Montessori Public (via search)](https://www.montessoripublic.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/11/MP_Articles-PDF_V71-Zoll_Saylor_Feinberg.pdf)

**Color-coded syllables / morphology**
- French master's thesis (65 second-grade dyslexic children): colorizing syllables or vowels did not change reading speed but improved accuracy; children preferred colored texts. Thesis, abstract-level. Label: WEAK. — [dumas.ccsd.cnrs.fr](https://dumas.ccsd.cnrs.fr/dumas-03282114/document)
- Colored onset-rime intervention (Hines, 2007; first graders at risk): limited transfer beyond instructed patterns. Kirby thesis on coloring word exteriors: calls for follow-up on coloring morphological elements, i.e., untested. Label: WEAK / NO EVIDENCE for morphology coloring. — [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/56103432.pdf); [core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/213462151.pdf)
- A review of five morphological-awareness training studies in dyslexia found suggestive benefit; none of the summaries mention color, so the benefit belongs to the instruction, not the coloring. Label: WEAK. — [search summary](https://uwo.ca/fhs/lwm/teaching/EBP/2019_20/BalcomONeill.pdf)

**Colored parts of speech for adult/typical reading**
- McConnell (2007, Univ. of Pittsburgh master's thesis): sentences with font color signaling part of speech were read faster in an experiment; thesis-level, sample and methods not checked. Label: WEAK. — [core.ac.uk/download/12209632.pdf](https://core.ac.uk/download/12209632.pdf)
- Buoy et al. (2026 arXiv preprint, not peer reviewed; Khmer and Japanese): Khmer (n=43) bolding content words raised keyword-memory accuracy from 58.2% to 61.5% (p=0.03; roughly +3 points), cloze comprehension improvement only in a text subset (up to +15 points, p=0.04; overall p=0.06), factual questions got worse (about -5 points), no reading-time cost. Japanese pilot (n=10): syntactic color-coding added ~8 s per text (p=0.07), improved inferential-question odds (OR 1.31, p=0.02), no recall change. Label: WEAK (preprint, non-Latin scripts, multiple subgroup analyses, MCQ measures). — [arXiv 2603.00688](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00688v1)

**L2 grammar and gender coding**
- Arzt & Kost (2016): beginning German learners; color-coding vs gendered-actor technique; color group slightly improved at delayed post-test while control dropped (details/N not verified). Label: WEAK. — [scite author page (search result)](https://scite.ai/authors/jessica-arzt-zxdy6Q)
- Kohler (Univ. of Kansas dissertation): color vs black-and-white German grammar materials; reported ~16% (classroom) and ~13% (lab) better performance for color groups. Label: WEAK (dissertation, percentages not standardized effects). — [KU ScholarWorks](https://kuscholarworks.ku.edu/handle/1808/10026)
- Nyikos (Purdue dissertation, n=135, intact classes, four conditions): color + picture best for German article recall; pictures alone least effective. Label: WEAK (quasi-experimental, old). — [Purdue](https://docs.lib.purdue.edu/dissertations/AAI8814499)
- Aljehani (Saudi EFL; color-coding for articles and quantifiers; mixed methods): author recommends color; effect sizes not reported in what I saw. Label: WEAK. — [Macrothink IJELE](https://macrothink.org/journal/index.php/ijele/article/view/19956)
- Indirect benchmark: meta-analyses of textual enhancement (color is one form) report small average effects (d about 0.30 across 45 studies; d about 0.22 in another), versus d about 0.93 for explicit rule presentation. Label: MODERATE that visual enhancement alone has small effects on L2 grammar. — [search summary of meta-analysis results](https://eprints.lancs.ac.uk/id/document/26274)
- Not found: Ramsey, Kuo, Sinclair, Lee studies named in the brief. I could not locate them; not reported.

### Inferences
- Color for grammar looks like a scaffold for instruction (explicit teaching + a consistent code), not a standalone reading aid. A passive "auto-colored prose" feature has no direct evidence for typical English readers.
- If a color code is used for learning (e.g., noun gender), expect modest gains (about 0.2-0.3 SD at best, by the textual-enhancement benchmark), which depend on the user being taught what the colors mean.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed controlled English-language study on coloring parts of speech for adult comprehension; Bolderson 2011 results not retrieved; Montessori controlled studies none found; the L2 studies I saw were not opened in full.

## 3. Syntax highlighting in programming

### Takeaway
Evidence for syntax highlighting is mixed and mostly small: faster task completion in one 10-person eye-tracking study (benefit shrinks with experience), but null results in the largest controlled experiment (390 novices) and in a student eye-tracking study. No study I found tests whether the effect transfers to prose.

### Cited Findings
- Hannebauer, Hesenius & Gruhn (2018, Empirical Software Engineering 23:2795-2828): controlled experiment, 390 undergraduates in an introductory Java course; each saw some tasks with and some without highlighting; no evidence that highlighting improved correctness on small coding tasks. Authors flag task choice as a limitation and note very few comparable experiments. Label: MODERATE (largest N, within-subject, null). — [dblp record](https://dblp1.uni-trier.de/rec/journals/ese/HannebauerHG18.html); abstract via [search result](https://ersearch2.cvtisr.sk/vufind/SummonRecord/FETCH-LOGICAL-a247t-4db215e272f99f82224cc0ae4a4ba84e74657cf98ecbca5de60d98be456d572b3/Details?lng=en)
- Sarkar (2015), "The impact of syntax colouring on program comprehension": eye tracking, 10 subjects, within-subjects, randomized; highlighting significantly improved task completion time, with the effect weaker at higher programming experience. Label: WEAK (n=10). I could not read the PDF text. — [advait.org PDF](https://advait.org/files/sarkar_2015_syntax_colouring.pdf); summary in [search results](https://advait.org/files/sarkar_2015_syntax_colouring.pdf)
- Beelders & du Plessis (J Eye Movement Research, Dec 2015, vol 9(1)): student programmers; fixation counts, durations and regressions were higher for black-and-white code than colored code but not significantly; students did not have significantly more difficulty with black-and-white code. Label: WEAK-MODERATE null. — [JEMR](https://bop.unibe.ch/JEMR/article/view/2429). A separate 2016 ACM paper by the same authors on scanning behavior was seen only as a citation. — [Crossref record](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1145%2F2987491.2987536)
- COMPSAC 2020 paper varied syntax highlighting and typeface with EEG-based cognitive load (findings not extracted). — [COMPSAC PDF](https://conferences.computer.org/compsac/pdfs/COMPSAC2020-7r2yh3mCdmuNATjWZbeuiI/730300b105/730300b105.pdf)
- Searches for "Schulze" and "Hansen" syntax highlighting studies returned nothing matching; Siegmund et al. fMRI work is about comprehension in general, not highlighting. Not reported further.
- Transfer to prose: no direct study of "syntax highlighting" of English prose; closest are the POS-styling items under section 2 (WEAK).

### Inferences
- Highlighting helps (if at all) with search/orientation and completion time for less-experienced users on specific tasks, which fits a "visual pop-out for navigation" account, not a "deeper comprehension" account. Inference only.
- Code has formal token classes, unambiguous roles and re-reading by design; prose does not. So transfer to prose should be assumed zero until tested.

### Gaps
- Effect sizes for Sarkar and Hannebauer not captured; Hansen and Schulze studies not found; no direct prose-transfer experiment.

## 4. Colored overlays and tinted lenses (Irlen, visual stress)

### Takeaway
This is the clearest myth in the set: the best systematic reviews conclude coloured lenses/overlays "cannot be endorsed" for reading difficulties; benefits are small, placebo-comparable, and vanish in lower-risk-of-bias studies. Pediatric/ophthalmology bodies explicitly do not endorse tinted lenses for learning disabilities. A user-selected tint is harmless but should not be marketed as a reading aid.

### Cited Findings
- Griffiths, Taylor, Henderson & Barrett (2016, Ophthalmic & Physiological Optics, systematic review): 51 published items (54 data sets) from PsycInfo/Medline/Embase, assessed with the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool; most had high/uncertain risk of bias; lower-risk studies showed less benefit; effects generally small and similar to placebo; results didn't differ by colour system (Intuitive, Irlen, Harris/Chromagen) or by visual stress vs reading difficulty classification; conclusion: use of coloured lenses or overlays to ameliorate reading difficulties cannot be endorsed; reported gains attributed to placebo, practice or Hawthorne effects. Label: STRONG (negative). Note: this is a systematic review using the Cochrane RoB tool, not a Cochrane Library review; I found no Cochrane Library review on this topic. — [White Rose eprint 104441](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/104441/)
- Albon, Adi & Hyde (2008, systematic review, cited by later work): evidence too low in quality to conclude on efficacy or cost-effectiveness of coloured lenses for reading disability (second-hand). Suttle et al. (2018) overview: 3 of 4 systematic reviews found insufficient good-quality evidence. — [search results incl. core.ac.uk](https://core.ac.uk/download/153514211.pdf); [Review of Optometry](https://reviewofoptometry.com/article/study-casts-doubt-on-colored-overlays-lenses)
- Henderson, Tsogka & Snowling (2013, J Research in Special Educational Needs 13:57-65): undergraduates with and without dyslexia read jumbled text faster with overlay, but overlays did not improve reading rate or comprehension of connected text; gains unreliable and reduced at retest. Label: MODERATE (negative, adults, small). — [White Rose eprint 85990](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/85990)
- Stagg & Kiss (2021; n=59 adolescents): overlays had no significant effect on speed or errors (see section 1). — [Westminster](https://westminsterresearch.westminster.ac.uk/item/x14xz/room-to-read-the-effect-of-extra-large-letter-spacing-and-coloured-overlays-on-reading-speed-and-accuracy-in-adolescents-with-dyslexia)
- AAP/AAO/AAPOS/AACO joint statement (Pediatrics 2009;124:837-844; technical report reaffirmed 2014): eye exercises, behavioral vision therapy and special tinted filters/lenses are not endorsed for learning disabilities for lack of scientific evidence; children with dyslexia have the same visual function and ocular health as others. (A "2011" date in the brief could not be confirmed.) Label: STRONG as an institutional position. — [AAO clinical statement](https://www.aao.org/clinical-statement/joint-statement-learning-disabilities-dyslexia-vis); [AAPOS glossary](https://aapos.org/glossary/learning-disabilities)
- Dyslexia and visual stress appear to be independent conditions; some individuals with both report benefit from overlays; evidence-based visual-stress claims remain contested. Label: WEAK. — [Frontiers in Psychology mini-review](https://frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00833/full)

### Inferences
- Do not present background-color or tint presets as treatments for dyslexia. Offering a user-controlled tint for comfort/preference is fine; call it preference, not therapy.

### Gaps
- Primary text of Albon 2008 not retrieved; the AAP "2011" document not located.

## 5. Bionic Reading, bolded word starts, RSVP/Spritz, BeeLine gradients

### Takeaway
Bionic-style bolding does not speed reading or improve comprehension in the three independent tests I found (one pre-registered, n=90); it only shifts where the eye first lands in long words. The marketed speed claims are vendor-originated and unsupported. RSVP/Spritz shows a speed-comprehension trade-off. BeeLine's gradient has one mixed peer-reviewed study in young children, otherwise vendor data.

### Cited Findings

**Bionic Reading**
- Parker, Lee, Wang & Joseph (2026, Royal Society Open Science, Registered Report; 13(9), rsos250177): 90 skilled adult readers (18-40), single sentences in 18-pt Courier New with a 4- or 8-letter target; unbolded vs start-bolded vs middle-bolded. Start-bolding shifted first landing position toward word start for 8-letter words (little effect for 4-letter); no meaningful differences in skipping, reading times or comprehension (about 95% correct, near ceiling); readability ratings favored unbolded (7.48 vs 6.76 start-bolded vs 5.43 middle-bolded; 43/90 chose unbolded). Individualized Bionic settings and dyslexic readers not tested. Label: STRONG-to-MODERATE (preregistered, adequate N, but single-sentence, short, skilled readers, ceiling on comprehension). Details come from a ScienceBlog write-up; the RSOS page returned 403; existence and conclusion confirmed by a University of Reading repository record. — [ScienceBlog write-up](https://scienceblog.com/s-bionic-reading-bolds-the-first-half-of-each-word-on-the-promise-of-more-efficient-reading-and-a-registered-eye-tracking-study-of-90-skilled-adult-readers-found-the-bolding-moved-their-first/); [Reading CentAUR](https://centaur.reading.ac.uk/130289/); [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.250177)
- Snell (2024, Acta Psychologica 247:104304), "No, Bionic Reading does not work": reading times for 100 paragraphs in Bionic vs normal font; no significant difference; author concludes Bionic Reading does not facilitate reading. Participant N not given on the page I could read. Label: MODERATE. — [VU Research](https://research.vu.nl/en/publications/no-bionic-reading-does-not-work/); [PubMed 38723450](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38723450/)
- Beelders (2025, Journal of Eye Movement Research 18(5):49): 53 adults, within-subjects, two Hemingway passages (Arial vs Bionic, Latin square, 600 Hz Tobii). Speed 131.1 vs 130.8 wpm (p=0.9); fixation duration 232.9 vs 228.4 ms (p=0.3); fixation counts n.s.; no preregistration mentioned; comprehension results not reported in the excerpt; effect sizes not reported; first-letter-only analysis. Label: MODERATE (null). — [PMC12565662](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12565662/)
- Readwise (July 2022; practitioner/vendor, self-selected online sample): 3,334 started, 2,074 finished, final n=1,916; two 1,000-word Paul Graham essays, Literata plain vs Bionic alternating; 325.3 wpm (Bionic) vs 327.9 wpm (plain); mean paired difference -2.6 wpm; paired t=-1.92, two-tailed p=0.055; comprehension 88% in both; 52% of readers faster with Bionic (+35 wpm) and 48% faster without (+43 wpm). Note the earlier search summary quoting one-tailed p=0.0275 for the same data. Label: WEAK-as-experiment (uncontrolled online) but informative: no average speed gain. — [Readwise blog](https://blog.readwise.io/bionic-reading-results/) (self-described company; hoped it would work)
- The vendor's own small study (reported as 12 readers, unpublished) and claims like "save up to 35% of reading time" come from marketing pages; no peer-reviewed support found. Label: NO EVIDENCE. — [search summary of Beelders/Readwise sources](https://blog.readwise.io/does-bionic-reading-actually-work/)
- Subjective reports of focus/line-tracking benefit for some ADHD or dyslexic readers are anecdotal community posts; not tested in the peer-reviewed studies above. Label: NO EVIDENCE (untested). — [okmiku blog (flagged: blog)](https://okmiku.com/blog/bionic-reading-focus-aid)

**RSVP / Spritz**
- Benedetto et al. (2015): RSVP/Spritz-style vs normal reading; no difference in inferential comprehension, lower literal comprehension with Spritz, at a modest rate. Schotter, Tran & Rayner (2014, UCSD; Psychological Science): RSVP-style reading that prevents regressions impairs comprehension. Di Nocera et al. (2018): no comprehension difference at lower RSVP speeds, drop at higher. Ricciardi et al. (2020): speed-comprehension trade-off from about 250 wpm for long texts. Label: MODERATE (consistent direction across several studies; Benedetto small). — [APS press release](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/speed-reading-apps-may-impair-reading-comprehension-by-limiting-ability-to-backtrack.html); [Not so fast reply (Benedetto)](https://www.datalearner.com/academic/journal-papers/0747-5632/volumes-and-issues/181/paper-detail/36580); [Di Nocera 2018](https://iris.uniroma1.it/retrieve/e3835320-f382-15e8-e053-a505fe0a3de9/Di-Nocera_Rapid_2018.pdf); [Ricciardi 2020](https://repositori.uji.es/xmlui/handle/10234/191546)

**BeeLine Reader**
- Koornneef & Kraal (2022, Computers in Human Behavior Reports): two computerized experiments, grades 2-3 (ages 6-9); reading-time advantage for BeeLine texts in grade 2 but not grade 3 with long lines and tight spacing; with age-appropriate short lines and generous spacing, grade 2 read slower with BeeLine. Authors urge formal testing before classroom use. Label: WEAK-MODERATE (mixed, children). — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/b48617b6416f4030ae9bf57ab79d1318)
- Vendor claims: internal analysis of ~20% mean speedup (60% of readers), a WestEd study of 530 seventh graders (results not seen), and a district report of "75% better" reading; no independent verification, and a Behavioral Scientist commentary says independent research is still needed on whether the gradient or expectation drives any effect. Label: NO EVIDENCE (vendor/practitioner). — [Behavioral Scientist](https://behavioralscientist.org/speed-reading-apps-open-research-questions/); [WestEd PDF hosted by RIF](https://www.rif.org/sites/default/files/documents/2019/10/24/Support_Materials/BeeLineWestEdStudyFinal.pdf) (not opened)
- No peer-reviewed eye-tracking study of the BeeLine gradient found; no peer-reviewed data from "Hartman and colleagues" found.

### Inferences
- Start-bolding changes where the eye lands (a real, mechanistic effect), but skilled readers already land near the word center, so there is no net efficiency gain; this supports treating bolding as an attention cue, not a processing aid.
- Variation across individuals (52/48 split in Readwise) is consistent with noise around zero rather than a subgroup effect; no evidence that a subgroup (e.g., ADHD/dyslexia) benefits.

### Gaps
- No studies in dyslexic or ADHD adults/children for Bionic; Snell sample size; full methods for Parker et al. (RSOS page blocked); no independent BeeLine eye-tracking.

## 6. Skimming, F-pattern, headings, bullets, bolded key terms, scannability

### Takeaway
Skimming of web pages is common and well documented by practitioner eye tracking, but the F-pattern describes where people look when scanning, not what improves comprehension; NN/g's "scannable writing" gains come from a single 1997 study that combined text changes, so it can't isolate the effect of bold/bullets/headings.

### Cited Findings
- NN/g (Nielsen, 2006): eye tracking of 232 users on thousands of pages; dominant scanning shape resembles an F (top horizontal scan, shorter second scan, left vertical scan); also E and inverted-L shapes; it is a rough shape, shifts with page elements, and describes scanning, not thoroughness. Practitioner research, not peer reviewed. Label: WEAK-MODERATE (large N, practitioner, descriptive). — [NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/f-shaped-pattern-reading-web-content-discovered/)
- Morkes & Nielsen (1997, Sun Microsystems; practitioner): in a Nebraska tourism site test, concise text improved measured usability 58%, scannable layout 47%, objective language 27%, all three combined 124%; the concise version had half the word count; 79% of users always scanned new pages, 16% read word by word. Single site, single test, usability measured (task time/errors/memory/subjective), and the "scannable" condition mixed headings, bullets, highlighted keywords and short paragraphs. Label: WEAK (practitioner, confounded, 1997). — [NN/g Alertbox](https://www.nngroup.com/alertbox/9710a.html); [summary](https://www.deceptive.design/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest): highlighting/underlining rated "low utility" for learning: no benefit beyond reading alone, may hinder inference-making, students often highlight poorly. Applies to reader-generated highlighting; applied to author/tool emphasis it is an analogy. Label: STRONG (review) for self-highlighting; none for auto emphasis. — [Duke Scholars record](https://scholars.duke.edu/publication/954654); [NOVA summary](https://www.pbs.org/wgbh/nova/article/the-science-of-smart-put-down-that-highlighter-the-best-and-worst-ways-to-learn)
- Isolation effect (von Restorff): highlighted material draws attention whether or not it is important; highlighting did not significantly improve recall in the review cited by an eye-tracking paper (N=12). — [arXiv 2404.05572](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.05572)

### Inferences
- Headings/bullets/bold are justified for *finding* things, not shown to improve *understanding* or retention. Treat as navigation aids.
- The "124%" is not a defensible effect estimate for any single feature.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed controlled experiment isolating headings vs bullets vs bold for comprehension was found in this pass; none for phone-specific skimming.

## 7. Emphasis density (percent of text emphasized)

### Takeaway
I found no study that manipulates the percentage of emphasized text and measures comprehension. The adjacent evidence says emphasis captures attention (isolation effect) without reliably improving recall, and that heavy start-bolding (about half of every word) gave no benefit and was rated less readable than plain text.

### Cited Findings
- Isolation/attention-capture without recall benefit: see section 6 (arXiv 2404.05572 review; N=12 ETRA experiment reporting about half of fixations falling in highlighted areas, reported second-hand). — [arXiv 2404.05572](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.05572)
- Parker et al. registered report: start-bolded text (emphasis on roughly half of every word) rated lower in readability than unbolded (6.76 vs 7.48); middle-bolded lowest (5.43); no performance gain. — [ScienceBlog write-up](https://scienceblog.com/s-bionic-reading-bolds-the-first-half-of-each-word-on-the-promise-of-more-efficient-reading-and-a-registered-eye-tracking-study-of-90-skilled-adult-readers-found-the-bolding-moved-their-first/)
- Leeds PhD thesis on italics (Norton, 2018) considers a "zero-impact" account of typographic emphasis (it may only "catch the eye"); and a font-emphasis L2 study (2019) found native-like processing of prominence cues. Details not extracted. — [White Rose eTheses](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/22135/1/Norton_CW_Languages_Cultures_and_Societies_PhD_2018.pdf)
- Buoy et al. (2026 preprint): bolding content words only (a subset of tokens) yielded small recall gains in Khmer, but factual questions got worse. — [arXiv 2603.00688](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.00688v1)
- Label for "emphasis density has an optimum": NO EVIDENCE (untested); the dilution idea ("if everything is bold nothing is") is a design principle, not an empirical result.

### Inferences
- Sparse emphasis on a small number of genuinely key items is consistent with the isolation-effect literature; dense emphasis likely removes the pop-out. Inference only.

### Gaps
- No direct dose-response study. This is a testable gap the tool could fill (A/B on percent emphasized).

## Implications for a text-styling tool

Evidence-labelled bullets. "[Evidence]" = directly supported by the cited findings; "[Inference]" = my reasoning, untested.

**What to build with reasonable support**
- [Evidence, MODERATE] Offer adjustable letter spacing (and word spacing) rather than a "dyslexia font." Spacing gains (accuracy mostly, speed inconsistently) replicate in dyslexic children (Zorzi 2012; Marinus 2016; Stagg & Kiss 2021; Hakvoort partial). Pair letter-spacing increases with word-spacing increases (Galliussi: speed suffered otherwise).
- [Evidence, MODERATE] Use a plain sans-serif/roman default; avoid italics for body text for readers with dyslexia (Rello & Baeza-Yates 2013). Do not claim OpenDyslexic/Dyslexie/Lexend help; independent data say no letter-shape benefit.
- [Evidence, WEAK-MODERATE] Allow larger sizes (about 18 pt equivalent) for dyslexic users; do not promise line-spacing benefits (Rello 2013 found none).
- [Evidence, STRONG small effect] For expository text under time pressure, paper beats screens for comprehension (Delgado 2018). The tool can't fix that; avoid claims of parity and consider "slow-mode" prompts as untested design.
- [Evidence, MODERATE] Default to dark-on-light (positive polarity) for small text; treat dark mode as a comfort/ambient preference (Piepenbrock/Buchner; NN/g).
- [Evidence, MODERATE] Line length: pick ~55-75 characters per line by default as a harmless convention; evidence says longer lines read at least as fast.

**What to avoid or label as unsupported**
- [Evidence, STRONG negative] Do not market colored overlays/tints as dyslexia or visual-stress aids (Griffiths 2016; AAP/AAO joint statement; Henderson 2013; Stagg 2021). A user-controlled tint is fine as "preference."
- [Evidence, MODERATE negative] Do not build "Bionic Reading" as a speed/comprehension feature (Parker 2026 registered report n=90; Snell 2024; Beelders 2025 n=53; Readwise n=1,916). Start-bolding shifts the first landing point only; users rated it less readable than plain text.
- [Evidence, MODERATE negative] Do not build RSVP/one-word-at-a-time as a comprehension tool; comprehension drops at higher speeds, especially for literal recall (Schotter/Rayner; Di Nocera; Ricciardi).
- [Evidence, NO EVIDENCE] Do not claim gradient-per-line (BeeLine) improves reading; only one mixed peer-reviewed child study exists; vendor numbers are unreplicated.
- [Evidence, STRONG for self-highlighting] Auto-highlighting large amounts of text is not a retention strategy (Dunlosky 2013); expect no memory benefit from emphasis alone.

**Color-coded grammar / syntax highlighting (the core "synesthetic layer" bet)**
- [Evidence, WEAK] Color-coding grammar has small-scale, mostly dissertation-level positive results for L2 learners (gender, articles) and small clinical studies for DLD (Colourful Semantics). Effect sizes sit around the textual-enhancement benchmark (d about 0.2-0.3) and the color is confounded with explicit instruction.
- [Evidence, WEAK] The only direct tests of POS-based styling of prose are a 2007 master's thesis (faster reading), and a 2026 non-peer-reviewed preprint in Khmer/Japanese (small gains in recall/inference, a factual-question cost). Not generalizable to English.
- [Evidence, MIXED] Syntax highlighting in code: null in the largest experiment (Hannebauer, n=390), null in Beelders & du Plessis, positive for completion time in a 10-person study that shrank with experience (Sarkar). No transfer-to-prose test exists.
- [Inference] Frame any POS/clause coloring as an optional learning scaffold with a visible legend, targeted at learners (L2, early grammar) rather than at fluent readers; hypothesis: benefits scale with how much the reader needs to attend to the coded dimension, and shrink with expertise (Sarkar's pattern).
- [Inference] Keep the number of color categories small and palettes color-vision-safe; none of the cited studies tested category count, so this is a design precaution, not evidence-based.

**Emphasis and structure**
- [Evidence, WEAK] Headings/bullets/short chunks are supported mainly as scanning aids (NN/g, practitioner); effects on comprehension and retention are unproven; the 124% figure is confounded.
- [Inference] If emphasizing, keep it sparse and semantically meaningful (key terms), because emphasis density is untested and the isolation effect suggests dilution. Build in logging/A-B so the tool can measure emphasis percentage vs comprehension itself (this is an open research gap).

**Overall stance**
- [Inference] Treat any style feature as *preference-and-affordance*, personalized and reversible, with explicit labels ("experimental") unless it falls in the supported list above (spacing, size, polarity-for-small-text, sans-serif). Avoid performance claims in the UI.
